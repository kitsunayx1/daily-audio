## Morning Signal. Tuesday, the twenty seventh of October.

Good morning. Tuesday. Israel is voting, Chrome is quietly evicting a certificate authority, and somewhere a law firm's help desk is taking a call from someone who isn't who he says he is.

This is Morning Signal for Tuesday, the twenty seventh of October. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about five yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief has every source link, and the organisations table has fifty five rows for your vendor and client lookups.

I begin with a correction, and it's a significant one. Yesterday I led with the first confirmed break-in using P H P two Shell, a European government body, with the intruder apparently an A I agent. Palo Alto's Unit forty two has since retracted the link to P H P two Shell. It says it's unable to confirm that the initial access had anything to do with those vulnerabilities. The intrusion itself still happened. Web shell, password hashes, multi factor secrets, a relay into Active Directory. What's gone is the explanation for how they got in.

I told you it with confidence, because they told it with confidence. That's the trouble with first reports. They arrive dressed for a party, and the facts turn up later in work clothes. I'd rather correct it than defend it.

Three things first.

## The top three

Number one. P H P two Shell is now officially exploited, and working shells have been seen.

Late yesterday, C I S A added both P H P two Shell vulnerabilities to its list of known exploited vulnerabilities, with a federal deadline of Thursday. Three days. That's short, even by C I S A's standards, and it comes with a requirement to do forensic triage, not just patch.

The evidence came from a researcher's honeypots. On Saturday, Adel Karimi recorded thirty five working shells across three decoy servers, almost all from a single address, using a four stage chain that matches a public proof of concept. Exhaust the memory. Leak some more. Then run a command. No second stage was downloaded. The intruders, it seems, were just checking that the door opened.

A fair caveat. The honeypots ran P H P in the configuration most favourable to the attacker, a single worker. That tells you the exploit works. It doesn't tell you how often it works elsewhere. But C I S A doesn't add things to that list on the strength of hope.

And the problem is spreading into places you don't usually think of as P H P. Industrial vendors are publishing advisories. Schneider Electric, Siemens, A B B, Johnson Controls, Belden. One of Belden's router lines has no patch at all, and the advice is to switch off the web interface. Censys counts more than thirty two million P H P services still on affected versions.

What to do. Patch, and restart the P H P workers, because a patch on disk does nothing to a process already running. Treat unpatched internet facing P H P as possibly compromised and follow C I S A's triage steps. Hunt for download tools spawned by the web server. And remember the marketing sites, the WordPress and Drupal estates that somebody's agency set up three years ago and nobody has visited since.

Number two. The help desk is the new front door, and law firms are the house everyone wants to visit.

Yesterday afternoon, Falcon listed Beasley Allen, a large American plaintiff litigation firm. It claims about two hundred and fourteen gigabytes. Settlement agreements, court signed stipulations, privileged internal memos, litigation strategy, rough trial transcripts. No statement from the firm. That's Falcon's second mass tort firm in two days, after Weitz and Luxenberg on Sunday.

Here's why the name matters. Google and GuidePoint say Falcon is a rebrand of a group Google tracks as U N C sixty six seventy one. Same people, new letterhead. Their method is wonderfully low tech. They phone your staff pretending to be your own I T help desk. They send them to a fake login page that captures the session, which gets them past multi factor authentication. Then they walk from Okta into Microsoft three sixty five and Salesforce, and leave with whatever's valuable. GuidePoint counts more than eighty three victims, eight or nine of them law firms, and more than eight million dollars paid. Since July, they've concentrated on private equity, law firms and anyone holding merger or litigation data.

Read that list again, slowly. Private equity. Law firms. Merger and litigation data. It's not a description of us. But it's not far off.

Meanwhile, a plaintiff firm has opened a class action investigation into White and Case, over Sunday's Qilin listing. A listing with no proof, from a group known to invent victims. The lawyers moved within a day. The forensics will take weeks. In that gap, a firm can lose a great deal of client confidence over something that may not have happened.

And the same theme turns up in today's deep dive. Arctic Wolf has analysed more than four hundred intrusions that began with a Microsoft Teams chat from someone pretending to be I T support. We'll come to it.

What to do. Tell staff, plainly and often, that the help desk never cold calls and never cold chats. Require a callback to a known number before anyone grants remote access. And restrict who outside the firm can start a Teams conversation with your people.

Number three. A I as the attacker is no longer a forecast.

In South Korea, police say a single suspect used generative A I to breach about ninety organisations and steal eight point two million records. Academies, hospitals, universities, golf courses, and an affiliate of a major corporation. Police say sixty of those intrusions were A I assisted, and thirty eight victims were extorted. He was arrested on the fifteenth. Five accomplices have followed. Six are still at large.

Then there's Cathedral, a start-up founded by former staff of DOGE, the American government efficiency office. In what both sides call an authorised test, Cathedral's A I agents reached Anthropic's sales, H R and I T data in under twelve hours. Not through Anthropic's own systems, but through flaws in third party administrative and sales software. Meeting transcripts, salary details, internal Slack messages. Anthropic says its monitoring caught some of it, access was cut the same day, and Cathedral didn't fully follow the rules of the bug bounty. No customer prompts, no model weights, no code. Still. Twelve hours, through the back office.

And OpenAI released a model it says meets the "Critical" threshold for cyber capability in its own safety framework. That means, in OpenAI's words, building working zero day exploits across hardened systems, or running a full attack from a high level goal. It's generally available, at half the price of its predecessor, with safeguards. Safeguards are like a good lock. They work best on people who weren't going to break in anyway.

What to do. Put A I driven intrusion into your tabletop exercises. Count the administrative and sales software in your vendor list as part of your attack surface, because the agents clearly do. And assume the time between "vulnerable" and "breached" is shrinking toward hours.

## Supply chain

The supply chain story of the day is a worm called testmetest. JFrog found it had armed forty two GitHub repositories belonging to eleven owners, starting as early as July. The payloads were wrapped in files of thirteen megabytes, too big for GitHub's code search to bother reading. Hiding in plain sight, by being boring and large.

What's new is where it hides. It plants configuration files for A I coding assistants. Claude, Gemini, Cursor, Visual Studio Code. Files that tell the assistant to run a script every time a session starts. Open the project, start your helpful assistant, and it runs the attacker's code before it says good morning.

And it carries a wiper. If the stolen GitHub token is revoked, it deletes the user's home directory. A dead man's switch. So the order of operations matters. Remove the persistence first, then revoke the token. Do it the other way round and you'll be restoring from backup, assuming you have one.

Elsewhere, malicious packages posing as Claude clients on the Python package index, harvesting A P I keys. And dependency confusion packages aimed at corporate namespaces, including one flagged as SiriusXM's. Someone is checking which internal package names companies forgot to reserve publicly.

## Deep dive: the Teams help desk

Today's deep dive is not a famous group. It's a technique, used by many, documented by Arctic Wolf across more than four hundred incidents. The fake I T help desk on Microsoft Teams.

It begins with noise. Your inbox floods. Newsletters, subscription confirmations, hundreds of them, in minutes. It's called an email bomb, and its purpose is not to deliver anything. It's to make you want help.

Then help arrives. A Teams chat, or a call, from "Help Desk", or "I T Support", or "I T Service Desk, Matt", or "Enterprise I T and Security, Internal." Sometimes with your own company's name in it. Sometimes with an emoji, because nothing says corporate I T like a small cartoon shield. Teams shows a warning that the sender is external. People are very good at not reading warnings when someone is offering to fix their inbox.

Matt is sympathetic. He'll sort it out. He just needs you to open Quick Assist, the remote support tool built into Windows, or install a remote management tool. And here's the professional touch. He never pastes a link into the chat, because Microsoft's link scanning might catch it. He browses to the payload himself, once he's on your screen.

What happens next is quick. Arctic Wolf saw several users targeted within about five minutes, and code running less than fifteen minutes after someone engaged. In its example, PowerShell pulled an installer from Microsoft's own Azure storage, which unpacked a Python implant that listed every user and computer in Active Directory and sent the list home. In some cases, alerts for Kerberoasting and Golden Ticket activity, which is to say, attacks on the domain's crown jewels, fired alongside the first alert about the Teams chat.

Who's doing it? Arctic Wolf says the activity aligns with a cluster Sophos calls S T A C forty seven forty nine, a financially motivated group that deployed Chaos ransomware against North American organisations earlier this year. The technique descends from Black Basta, and Cactus and 3AM affiliates have used it too. It's not a nation. It's a trade.

The infrastructure is evolving. Early on, the fake help desks came from free trial Microsoft tenants, the ones ending in "on microsoft dot com". Those are now only a third or so of cases. The rest use paid domains, many on the dot top domain, chosen because they're cheap and nobody asks questions.

For persistence, Sophos saw startup entries disguised as Realtek audio drivers, which nobody has ever looked at twice, and shortcuts named like Windows security or OneDrive. And a second remote management tool, installed for redundancy, in case you find the first.

Why this matters for us in particular. We federate Teams with clients constantly. Our people are used to chatting with outsiders, and used to being helpful. That's a virtue in consulting, and a vulnerability in security.

How to look for it. In the Microsoft audit log, look for chats created by external or trial tenants with display names mentioning help desk, I T or support, and correlate them with a surge of email to the same person in the previous half hour. On endpoints, look for Quick Assist or any remote management tool starting shortly after an external Teams chat, for installers pulled from Azure blob storage, and for Python running from a user's own folders. Look for startup entries named Realtek or WinAudio. And watch for Kerberoasting after any remote assistance session.

How to stop it. Restrict Teams external access to an allow list of domains, and block trial tenants outright, there's a single setting for it. Turn on external sender warnings. Remove Quick Assist where you don't need it, and allow only the remote tool your help desk actually uses. Then the human part. Make it policy that the help desk never initiates contact through external Teams chat, and that any request for remote access gets a callback to a known number. And if someone did let Matt in: isolate the machine, revoke sessions, reset credentials, including service accounts, look for the second remote tool, and if there's any sign of Golden Ticket activity, rotate the domain's master key twice.

The lesson is the oldest one in the book. The most dangerous person in any building is the one who arrives at exactly the moment you need help, and offers it.
