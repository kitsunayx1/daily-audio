## Morning Signal. Tuesday, the seventeenth of November.

Good morning. Yesterday, for about seventeen hours, some of the most visited websites in the world served malware to their own visitors, and not one of them had been hacked. The code came from Optimizely, a service they all trust to run experiments on their pages. In Japan, the customer experience platform that switched itself off on Friday has begun to tell us what it lost, and its customers have begun to tell us what that means. And Google says it has now watched a machine find a flaw, write the exploit, and use it, with no human in the loop at all.

This is Morning Signal for Tuesday, the seventeenth of November. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about half past five yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief carries every source link, and the organisations table has forty seven rows.

Corrections first. On Monday I said Plaid began suspending KARTE at about a quarter to one on Friday morning, Eastern. Plaid's own notice now says it detected the intrusion a little before seven in the evening on Thursday, Eastern, and moved the whole platform into maintenance at about twenty past twelve on Friday morning. A few minutes earlier than I said. On Uber, I gave you state counts in the low thousands. The Texas filing now puts the total at one hundred and forty nine thousand, three hundred and forty six people. And on N able, one outlet gives the new chain its own vulnerability number. Rapid Seven says it has none, and that it rides on a flaw in Apache Axis. I am going with Rapid Seven.

Three things.

## The top three

Number one. Optimizely. Beginning late on Sunday night, Eastern, an attacker wrote code into the project settings of Optimizely Web customers. Optimizely then served that code, from its own content network, under its own certificate, to everyone who visited those sites. It swapped cryptocurrency addresses as people pasted them. From the afternoon, it skimmed payment cards. It stole cookies and took screenshots. And it sent iPhones to exploit kits. Report URI counted one hundred and seventy eight organisations, and calls that a floor: the B B C, C N N, Zoom, H S B C, Salesforce, Equifax. In professional services, the law firm Freshfields, the accounting firm CohnReznick, and the consultancy Baringa. No McKinsey, no Big Four, no Accenture on the list. Optimizely blames a flaw in its account signup flow. Nobody has been named. This is the deep dive, and the question for this morning is whether anything we, or our clients, run loads Optimizely.

Number two. Plaid KARTE. Plaid now confirms that seventy nine thousand account email addresses leaked, along with the keys and tokens its customers used to connect KARTE to everything else, and part of the customer data those customers had uploaded. The clients have started to speak. Francfranc, up to nearly six million member records. Ainz and Tulpe, two point six million. Shikoku Electric, about six hundred thousand, which is every member it has. And the RIKEN supercomputer breach now has a door, a public status page, and a number: four thousand five hundred and ninety seven users whose keys and credentials were taken, and already tried elsewhere.

Number three. Google's threat intelligence group says automated exploitation has arrived, and that a suspected North Korean group has been planting backdoors in Claude Code configuration files. Meanwhile an extortion crew called N zero n posted about sixty names in twenty minutes, Visa, Mastercard, JPMorgan, Walmart, a US law firm and a dozen Canadian financial advisers among them, which looks a great deal like one vendor, not sixty intrusions. And a Confluence flaw is being exploited and is not yet in the government's catalogue.

## Breaches and confirmed incidents

Japan, where Friday's switch off has become this week's disclosure season.

Plaid's account is now fuller. In the early hours of Friday, Tokyo time, a third party exploited a vulnerability in one of its servers, took the credentials KARTE uses to reach its own systems and databases, and pulled data out. What left: account email addresses, external integration keys and tokens, and part of the customer data that client companies had uploaded. KARTE remains fully suspended with no date to come back. The thing to understand is that the tokens are the dangerous part. A stolen email list is a nuisance. A stolen set of keys into a client's other systems is an invitation.

So to the clients. Francfranc, the homewares retailer, says up to about five point nine eight million member records, names, dates of birth and municipality, and about four million purchase history records. No cards, no passwords. Ainz and Tulpe and its sister brand AYURA, up to about two point six million members combined. Shikoku Electric Power says about six hundred thousand, every registered member of its customer portal. The apparel group World, the font maker Morisawa with about twenty four thousand buyers, the mobile carrier mineo, the outdoor brand Goldwin, the crowdfunding site CAMPFIRE, and the publisher Shueisha have all disclosed or suspended services. One headline puts the eyewear chain JINS at more than eighteen million records. I could not open it, and I will not repeat a number like that as fact until I can.

RIKEN, which runs the Fugaku supercomputer, says the attackers came in through a public web status dashboard between the tenth and the thirteenth, then moved to servers holding user data and installed malware. Some users' private S S H keys and credentials left the building. Four thousand two hundred and twenty two Fugaku users, and three hundred and seventy five on a smaller system. The awkward detail is that the stolen credentials include ones for supercomputers and services RIKEN does not run, and the attackers have already tried them. Toyota and Sumitomo Rubber, both users, have been notified. Researchers are a trusting people. They reuse keys the way the rest of us reuse coffee cups.

The Fireworks A I chain has reached Japan too. SmartHR, the H R software company, and pixiv, the art platform, say Notion A I chat records may have leaked, from the window in October we have discussed before. Bandai Namco's logistics arm reports about four hundred and thirteen thousand prize lottery records through a misused vendor account. Nissin Foods, through the same LINE integration platform that hit Keihan on Monday, reports one point three seven million follower records. And Nulab's diagramming service Cacoo has been fully down since Saturday after its own credentials were misused.

Korea. The office messenger from Daou, a groupware vendor, pushed a malicious automatic update to some Windows machines from Sunday afternoon to Monday morning, local time. Saved browser passwords and Daou credentials may have been taken, and there are traces of remote desktop use. The clean version is four point five point twelve. Mac and mobile are unaffected. An actor calling itself KimJEN claims data from twenty eight universities through a shared research administration system. One, Dong eui University, has confirmed five hundred and thirty people. The ticketing provider accesso exposed annual pass holders at Legoland Korea and SEA LIFE Busan, and since accesso sells to Merlin worldwide, other venues may follow. And Metaem, the A I contact centre vendor from Monday, has its first named client: Mega Coffee, with thirty phone numbers. Small, but now it has a name.

Europe. Trezor, the hardware wallet maker, says its shipping partner Skladon was breached, exposing about twenty seven thousand orders: names, phones, addresses and what they bought. No seed phrases. Some customers report fraudulent phone calls already, which is what a list of people who own cryptocurrency wallets is for.

And in the United States, Infoblox, the network security company, confirmed unauthorised access through what it calls a compromised third party data migration vendor. The Register reports the vendor did SharePoint migrations. What was exposed includes some customers' N I O S configuration backups, which describe their internal D N S and D H C P in detail, and employees' social security numbers and direct deposit details. Infoblox says its products and build pipeline were not involved. Think about what a D N S configuration backup is. It is a map of the building, drawn by the people who built it.

Two legal footnotes. Lexitas, a court reporting firm, is being sued over a misconfiguration that left files exposed for about seven years, affecting fourteen thousand people. And an unnamed consumer protection law firm faces a proposed nationwide class action in Georgia over an October breach.

## Exploited vulnerabilities

The catalogue did not move. One thousand seven hundred and eighty one entries, the same as Friday. Apple has a deadline tomorrow, SonicWall on Friday, and four more next Tuesday.

What should be in it, and is not, is a flaw in Apache Axis, an old web services library that still lives inside Atlassian's Confluence Data Center. A crafted message makes it load an attacker's Java class before anyone checks who is asking. Critical, nine point eight. Rapid Seven saw it exploited against a Confluence customer last Friday. It affects Confluence Data Center versions one through nine when the remote A P I is switched on, which it has not been by default for a very long time, but which older integrations still need. The fix is nine point two point twenty eight, or version ten, which removed the feature altogether. If you cannot patch, switch the remote A P I off. The brief tells you how to check.

The same Axis flaw is the front door to the N able chain from last week. N able says its next hotfix removes the legacy A P I permanently. Its hosted servers were upgraded yesterday. The on premises release is still to come. Keep the legacy A P I off.

Huntress has a story about ScreenConnect, distinct from the toolkit we covered last week. A flaw fixed in September lets a guest session push a file that an unpatched technician's client will run. Since the twentieth of October, somebody has sent about six hundred and eighty eight thousand such requests across forty two managed service provider servers. On the twelfth, one landed. A batch file became a rogue remote access client on a technician's machine in about nine seconds. The lesson Huntress draws is the one that matters: updating the ScreenConnect server is not enough. The fix lives in the technician's client, which must be on twenty six point six point five or later.

Check Point has published its fixed versions for the gateway flaw that was due yesterday, and says a couple of dozen customers were exploited. Some older releases will get no fix. SignDrop still has no patch. And on refrigeration, Midnight Blue has found six unpatched flaws in Danfoss supermarket controllers that allow takeover without a login. No exploitation reported. Take management interfaces off the internet, and keep them off.

## A I attacks and trends

Google's threat intelligence group published its quarterly A I threat report yesterday, and its headline is careful but plain. It has seen threat actors fully automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation without a human in the loop, for the first time. The tool, called exploit agent, sat on an exposed staging server. It took a vendor's patch, compared the code before and after, worked out what had been fixed, wrote an exploit for the unpatched version, and ran it. The notes were in Russian. The victims were French media, entertainment and education sites.

The report has more. Open source attack frameworks, CAIRN, which was our deep dive on Friday, and one called PentAGI, have been used in real intrusions, CAIRN against an unnamed US aerospace organisation. Zero day exploitation is up: about eight a month last year, about eleven a month this year, and twenty two in August alone. Stolen A I access is being resold through what Google calls L L M mixers, and some victims have been billed more than a hundred thousand dollars in a day.

And the item I want you to remember. A group Google calls MIDNIGHT NEPTUNE, suspected North Korean, went after cryptocurrency firms with fake recruiter profiles. Once inside, it modified the Claude Code settings file in internal code repositories, so that the next developer to launch Claude Code in that repository downloaded a backdoor called SOMBERMEME. Read that again. Not a malicious package. A configuration file, sitting in a repository, that a trusted tool reads and obeys. The practical step is to treat A I tool configuration in repositories as code: reviewed, owned, and watched for changes.

On ARTEX, last week's subject, CrowdStrike reported on Friday, and we had not yet covered it, that a China nexus espionage group it calls VAPOR PANDA used ARTEX against more than two hundred organisations in at least fourteen countries in late October, foreign ministries, a European presidency, American pharmaceutical, semiconductor, defence and nuclear organisations, with confirmed post compromise activity at nine. Yesterday a South Korean foreign policy think tank was added to the list. Indicators from SentinelOne and Cisco Talos are in the brief. Barracuda adds that its intruder went through about ten cloud accounts in forty four hours and left a Kubernetes backdoor.

And Hudson Rock found a market selling stolen browser logs with A I written profiles of each victim, searchable in plain English. A quarter of a dollar a log. The economics of crime have discovered the product description.

## Deep dive: the tag that turned

Let me tell you how one hundred and seventy eight websites were robbed without being broken into.

Optimizely Web is an experimentation service. A marketing team wants to know whether a green button sells better than a blue one, so it adds a small script from Optimizely to its pages, and from then on Optimizely decides, visitor by visitor, which version to show. To make that work, the script can run custom code that the customer writes in a field called Project JavaScript. The customer writes it. Optimizely compiles it and serves it from its own network. The customer's site trusts Optimizely completely, because that is the point.

At twenty one minutes past four on Monday morning, U T C, which is late on Sunday night in Washington, someone wrote into that field in the B B C's project. Over the following hours the same happened at Zoom, at C N N, at H S B C, at Salesforce, at Equifax, at IKEA, at GitLab, at Air France K L M, and, among the professional firms, at Freshfields, CohnReznick and Baringa. By the time Scott Helme of Report URI swept the snippets that evening, one hundred and eighty six of the one thousand six hundred and eleven he checked were infected. By the end, one hundred and eighty eight scripts at one hundred and seventy eight organisations. He calls that a floor.

What the code did changed through the day. The attacker rebuilt the payload twenty six times. It began as a cryptocurrency clipper, quietly replacing wallet addresses as people pasted them, across eight different chains, with the checksums done properly. In the early afternoon, U T C, a card skimmer was added, reading the site's own payment fields and sending what it found out quietly. Fields hosted by a payment provider in their own frame, Stripe Elements for example, were out of its reach, which is a small advertisement for that design. It stole any cookie not protected from scripts, and took screenshots of the page. And in the late afternoon came the most serious piece: visitors on older iPhones were routed, invisibly, to public exploit kits called Coruna and DarkSword. Report URI counts about seven thousand dollars arriving in the attacker's wallets. The money is the least of it. The iPhone exploitation is the part with a long tail.

Cleanup came in a rush, in about half an hour in the evening. One snippet, TalkTalk's, was still infected at nearly ten at night, U T C. Smartsheet was re infected once after being cleaned, which tells you the access had not been closed. A little after one in the morning, U T C, Optimizely posted a notice: code removed, accounts secured, protections added. Its spokesperson said the flaw was in the Experimentation account signup flow. That raises the obvious question. How does a flaw in signing up for a new account give you write access to the B B C's project? Optimizely has not said. Report URI, for its part, says plainly that it does not know how the attacker got in, and that the content network served exactly what it was given.

It is worth pausing on the iPhone piece. The router script checked the version of the phone. Versions thirteen to seventeen went to one kit, version eighteen to another, and the newest phones were only fingerprinted, which suggests the attacker had nothing that worked against them yet, or was saving it. A visitor reading the news on an old phone over breakfast would have seen nothing at all. That is the quality that makes this kind of attack expensive to clean up: the victims are not the companies whose names are on the list, but their visitors, and nobody has a list of those.

There was also a rehearsal. In the three days before, the same injector turned up in scripts from three smaller marketing vendors, Kissmetrics, StorifyMe and Minute Media. Nobody joined those dots until afterwards. They rarely do.

Who did it? Nobody knows. The injector, a script at a domain called webshell dot lol, was seen on Turkish websites in September and abusing three other marketing scripts in the days before. The iPhone loader is byte identical to one seen in a different campaign in October. The motive is money. Confidence in identity is low, and I would leave it there.

Why does this matter to a firm like ours? Because it is the fourth time in a week that one platform became the path into all its tenants. The Fireworks key, the Barracuda key, the ConnectWise backup application, and now a marketing tag. The firm's own sites, and the sites it builds for clients, are full of third party scripts that run with the full authority of the page, and are approved once and rarely read again.

So, what to do. First, inventory every Optimizely snippet the firm or its clients serve, including any copies proxied through your own domains. Second, read the project code and any experiment custom code in full. Do not just search for the attacker's domain, because one variant was Base sixty four encoded and will slip past a simple search. Look for decoding and eval calls, and for the runtime markers in the brief. Third, search proxy, D N S and content security policy logs from early Monday, U T C, for the domains and addresses listed. Blocked requests still tell you who was exposed. Fourth, use device management to find iPhones below eighteen point seven point three, or twenty six point three, that reached the routing domains, and update them. Fifth, treat any card entered on a first party form on Monday, and any session cookie readable by scripts, as exposed. And then, rotate Optimizely credentials and review who and what can write to your projects.

The longer remedy is unglamorous. A content security policy that says exactly where a page's scripts may send data. Report URI found that a restrictive policy blocked this payload on only fifteen percent of the affected sites it crawled. The other eighty five percent had either no policy, or one generous enough to let it through. A tag manager is a stranger you have given the keys to every room. Most days the stranger is honest. Monday, someone else was holding the keys.
