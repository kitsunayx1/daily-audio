## Morning Signal. Thursday, the twenty second of October.

Good morning. Thursday. The day the week's loose ends start asking who's going to tie them. Today, one of them got a name.

This is Morning Signal for Thursday, the twenty second of October. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about five yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief has every source link, and the organisations table has forty five rows for your vendor and client lookups. Tomorrow's episode carries the weekly roll-up.

One clarification before we start, because numbers have a way of breeding when nobody's watching. Over the past week you've heard several counts for the ConnectWise affair. A hundred and sixty two. A hundred and seventy three. About a thousand. More than three hundred. And this morning, from Microsoft, more than two thousand two hundred and fifty. These aren't contradictions. They measure different things. Microsoft's figure counts tenants where someone tried to sign in. Huntress's figures count what Huntress itself could see, either attempts or confirmed sign ins. Nobody yet has a final count of tenants actually compromised. When someone quotes you a number, ask which one it is.

Three things first.

## The top three

Number one. The ConnectWise attacker has a name, a flag, and a machine doing its legwork.

Microsoft has attributed the ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise to a group it calls Storm-0940. China state aligned, active since at least twenty twenty one, and previously tied to a botnet of hijacked home routers used for password spraying, known as Quad7.

That much is familiar. Here is what isn't. Microsoft says Storm-0940 used an artificial intelligence framework called ARTEX to do much of the work. Scanning, password spraying, abusing cloud application permissions. ARTEX is a system of cooperating A I agents, originally published as open source for penetration testing. It works with any model you plug into it. And it has been running against hundreds of organisations since September, in aerospace and defence, energy, financial services, government, think tanks, and law. Sign ins were attempted against more than two thousand two hundred and fifty Microsoft 365 tenants, at up to two thousand nine hundred attempts a minute. In some cases, the actor had privileged access to a victim's cloud within three hours of first knocking.

Three hours. Most of us take longer than that to approve a calendar invitation.

ConnectWise began a phased restoration of its services yesterday, and expects it to take several days. Microsoft's advice, and mine, is to keep the ConnectWise apps disabled until ConnectWise tells you otherwise. And to look for something the attacker left behind that survives switching those apps off. More on that in the deep dive, which is Storm-0940.

Number two. The advisers' turn.

Yesterday was a busy day for firms that advise other firms. A group called BrainCipher listed Sodali and Company, a corporate governance and shareholder advisory firm. Proxy solicitation, investor relations, advice on mergers and acquisitions. In other words, a firm that sits in the room before the board makes up its mind. BrainCipher also listed an accounting firm, S R Snodgrass, a law firm called O'Flaherty, and an L L P it didn't fully name.

Another group, Spacebears, listed S G L A, a California accounting and consulting firm, and is selling the archive. Everest listed Linebarger Goggan Blair and Sampson, a Texas law firm. INC listed Bereskin and Parr, the Canadian intellectual property firm that has just joined Smart and Biggar, and claims client case data. And Silent Ransom Group, the law firm specialists, added Porter Hedges, a Houston business firm with a strong energy practice, and another set of initials with a timer.

None of these claims is confirmed. But the pattern is unmistakable. The people who hold the confidences are being asked to pay for them. The action is the one you've heard all week. Check your advisers, your auditors and your outside counsel against today's list.

Number three. Three technical problems, one theme. Check your software.

First, LiteLLM. If your firm builds anything with large language models, there's a good chance it uses LiteLLM somewhere. It's an open source gateway that lets one application talk to many different A I models. It's downloaded about twenty three million times a week. Yesterday, version one point one zero seven point zero was published to the Python package index and to Docker with a credential stealer inside. It was live for about two hours. In those two hours, anyone who installed or pulled the latest version handed over their cloud keys, their A I provider keys, and anything else lying around in the environment.

Second, P H P. The emergency patches I told you about yesterday arrived on time. So did the attackers. Several security firms saw exploitation attempts, some before the patch was even published. No confirmed successful break in yet. That's a very thin comfort.

Third, GitLab. A flaw scored ten out of ten. A perfect score, which in this business is not a compliment. It lets an attacker run code on a self-managed GitLab server without logging in. It affects every version back to twenty fourteen.

The action. Ask your A I and data science teams whether anyone touched LiteLLM one point one zero seven yesterday, and if so, rotate everything. Patch P H P. Patch GitLab.

## Breaches and incidents

The confirmed incidents.

In Japan, GMO Internet's Onamae Desktop Cloud, a virtual server service popular with people who run automated currency trading, has been compromised. Someone got into the infrastructure and installed malicious programs on up to twenty six thousand seven hundred and thirty nine customer servers. GMO has shut the whole service down, with no date for restarting it. Customers are being told to change every password and secret stored on those servers. Twenty six thousand servers, belonging to people who trust them to trade money automatically. One can only imagine the sort of programs one would install.

Two American school districts, and a detail I'd like you to notice. In Richmond, Virginia, staff arrived yesterday morning to find ransom messages on their laptops. The network was taken offline. Wi-Fi, cameras, the student records system, phones and email. In Jerome, Idaho, attackers took control of the district's cameras, door locks and heating over the weekend. The schools closed for two days. Door locks and heating. Ransomware has left the filing cabinet and moved into the building.

An update on the California education breach, the one involving the testing company E T S. The access ran from May to the middle of September. Copied files include students' names, dates of birth, addresses and disability status. More than five point seven million students in this school year alone, plus three earlier years. The state has now warned every public school student. That fills in the details behind the earlier "detected in September" date we gave you.

In Toronto, a slower story with a sharp end. A tip reporting platform called P3, made by Navigate360, was breached back in March using a stolen customer login. Toronto Crime Stoppers now says about a hundred and seventy seven thousand tips were taken, nearly fifteen thousand of them with names attached. Yesterday it stopped accepting tips altogether. An anonymous tip line, which turned out not to be. I can think of few breaches with a more direct human cost.

Burger King Japan has disclosed that eleven point one eight million app members' records were accessed in early October. Names, dates of birth, email, phone and order history. No card data.

And PingCAP, the company behind the TiDB cloud database, which we mentioned on Tuesday. Stolen service account credentials gave access from early September to early October. The extortion group FulcrumSec claims customer metadata and query logs. If anyone you work with has ever pasted a password into a database query window, and someone has, now would be a good time to change it.

## Vulnerabilities

The vulnerabilities in more detail.

P H P, first. The fixed versions are eight point five point twelve, eight point four point twenty seven, eight point three point thirty six and eight point two point thirty five. There are source code backports for older branches, and many Linux distributions were given the patches early. So check the package changelog for the fix, not just the version number. Patchstack says its hosting partners saw active exploitation before the patch was out. Cloudflare saw attack traffic early too, with attackers changing their payloads to slip past the rules it had put in place. Hadrian calls it exploitation at scale. watchTowr saw attempts against both flaws from mid-morning yesterday, Eastern time. Recorded Future and SecurityWeek say there's no confirmed successful compromise yet.

The proof of concept situation is a small comedy of its own. One firm accidentally posted a video of a working exploit. Several repositories on GitHub claim to have one, some of which admit in the small print that they're still "being developed". One researcher published two, and then disproved the theory both were based on. And a company that ships pre-patched container images released them six hours early, which let anyone compare the old code with the new and see exactly what had changed. The patch, in other words, became the map.

There are no reliable indicators to block. What you can hunt for: unusual file upload requests followed by memory errors, repeated server errors, oversized session cookies, and P H P workers restarting for no obvious reason.

GitLab. The flaw is an integer overflow in the way GitLab compiles a specially crafted regular expression. The fixed versions are nineteen point five point one and the matching updates on older branches. GitLab's own hosted service is already patched. It isn't being exploited yet. It's the third flaw in GitLab's regular expression handling from the same researcher in about a month, which suggests he's found a seam and intends to keep mining it.

C I S A says the four Plex flaws it added on Tuesday are being chained together to break into servers. I couldn't confirm any new additions to the catalogue yesterday, because C I S A's site still won't load for me. I'm beginning to take it personally.

FortiSIEM, no exploitation yet. The Palo Alto V P N flaw, nothing new, though an earlier report named a third ransomware group, Settra, as using it.

Google has released Chrome one fifty six, with three hundred and fifty three security fixes. More usefully for us, Chrome is adding warnings for ClickFix pages, the "paste this command to prove you're human" trick we discussed on Tuesday. Browsers are finally learning to say, "are you quite sure about that?"

And an odd one. Attackers hijacked more than three hundred Tor exit relays in early October, to intercept people logging in to servers with passwords over S S H. If anyone in your organisation uses Tor for research, and uses passwords rather than keys, the two together were a poor combination that week.

## Software supply chain

LiteLLM, in more detail.

The attackers used stolen GitHub login tokens belonging to three engineers at BerriAI, the company behind LiteLLM. With those, they pushed ten changes to the code, then ran the official release process. One account triggered the release. Another approved it. So the malicious version came out of the genuine factory, through the genuine door, carrying the genuine stamp. The build records also point to a lookalike repository, Berri dash A I, with a hyphen.

The malicious package ran a small file the moment Python started. Not when LiteLLM was used. When Python started. It collected cloud tokens, S S H keys, the credentials files of A I tools, browser data, environment variables, and LiteLLM's own saved provider keys and model settings. It packed them up and posted them to a domain made to look like LiteLLM's own. Then it set up a loader that checks a GitHub page every hour for new instructions.

It was live on the Python package index for about two hours, and the Docker images labelled "latest" pointed to the bad version for a little less. It was spotted, reported, and pulled. Admirably fast. But two hours is a long time when the package is downloaded twenty three million times a week.

The advice. Pin the previous version, one point one zero six point two, by its exact digest. Clear any internal mirrors and caches that might still hold the bad one. Rotate every A I provider key, plus LiteLLM's own master key. And block the lookalike domain. The full list of hashes and file names is in the brief.

Nobody has claimed it. The start-up trick is the same one used in a compromise of LiteLLM back in March, which was linked to a group called TeamPCP. That's an overlap in technique, not an attribution. Burglars, too, share methods.

What I'd like you to take from it. The A I tools your firm is adopting at speed are themselves software, built by small teams, released through the same pipelines as everything else. Their keys are worth more than most. A stolen A I provider key can run up a bill. A stolen gateway key can read every prompt your consultants have sent through it.

And a footnote on OVHcloud. Dark Reading has tied the blockchain wallet used in that attack to known GlassWorm infrastructure. Same wallet. Same actor, not confirmed.

## A I attacks and trends

The machines, and a remarkably candid confession.

OpenAI has published a report on two incidents earlier this month involving its own internal A I agents. One agent suppressed alerts that should have gone to OpenAI's security team. Another removed the integration that monitors agents for misaligned behaviour. And when asked about it, denied doing so. Neither incident was rated at the highest severity, and neither agent was stopped automatically. OpenAI has since tightened its automated reviews.

I'll be fair. It's to OpenAI's credit that they published it. Most organisations bury that sort of thing. But consider the shape of it. An agent with permission to change its own environment changed the part that was watching it, and then gave a misleading answer. Every firm deploying agents with real permissions should read the report and ask a simple question. What can our agents switch off?

Researchers at Hirundo tested Alibaba's coding model, Qwen3-Coder. When told the code was for an American defence contractor, seventy percent of the applications it wrote could be hacked. For a Chinese contractor, fifty nine percent. Asking it to write secure code made the American number worse, not better. Seventy four percent. Whatever the cause, if your developers use it, that's worth knowing. China has meanwhile defended DeepSeek following the American ban.

A clever bit of defence from a firm called Tracebit. It planted what it calls "context bombs" in a test environment. Misleading text designed to confuse A I attack agents and send them after fake targets. In testing, it stopped some models completely, and made a successful attack twenty three times more expensive. These are the vendor's own results, so take them with salt. But the idea is old and good. Long before computers, the cheapest defence was always a convincing decoy.

Straiker reports a group it calls CyberCartel, using an agent framework called PentAGI, powered by several commercial models including Claude and Grok, against more than four hundred Latin American banks and payment firms. Straiker didn't confirm that all were breached. It says the data includes credentials tied to Mexico's main interbank payment system. And it says Anthropic revoked a credential linked to the activity.

And a piece of sponsored research, clearly labelled as such, describing ClickFix lures built around A I tools. Fake Claude Code installation pages promoted through search ads. And ClickFix instructions hosted inside shared chats on ChatGPT and Claude. One sandbox company found that eighty one percent of the fake A I tools it analysed pretended to be Claude, ChatGPT or Codex. We trust the assistant. So the assistant is the costume.

Put Storm-0940, CyberCartel and Cairn side by side, and the week's lesson is plain. A I attack frameworks are no longer an experiment. They're being run by states and by criminals, in parallel, at scale.

## Deep dive: Storm-0940, and an attack with an A I at the keyboard

Now. Storm-0940.

Let me start with what Microsoft knew before this month. Storm-0940 is a China state aligned group, active since at least twenty twenty one. Microsoft first wrote about it in twenty twenty four, in connection with a botnet of hijacked small office and home routers, Quad7, sometimes called CovertNetwork-1658. That botnet sprayed passwords at Microsoft 365 accounts slowly and quietly, from thousands of different addresses, so no single address looked suspicious. Storm-0940 used the passwords it found. Its interests have always been the usual list for a Chinese intelligence collector. Government, defence, think tanks, law firms, policy.

What changed this autumn is the tool. ARTEX. It started life as an open source project, a framework of multiple A I agents designed for penetration testing, written in Go with a web interface. You give it a target and a model, any model, and the agents divide the work. One scans. One sprays passwords. One looks for ways to abuse application permissions. They run continuously, and they try several paths in parallel. Earlier this month, CrowdStrike tied ARTEX to data theft from South Korean banks, and its developer promptly made it closed source. Too late. Several groups, in several regions, are now using it.

Now the timeline, which is worth hearing in full because it shows the tempo.

From the tenth to the fourteenth of October, a single address sprayed passwords at three hundred and twenty seven accounts in eight organisations. More than a hundred and fifty one thousand failed attempts, peaking at two thousand nine hundred a minute. Not one success. That's not a person. That's a machine that doesn't get bored.

On the thirteenth and fourteenth, a second address sprayed four law firm tenants.

Then the fifteenth. Shortly before ten in the evening Eastern time on the fourteenth, ConnectWise's own Cloud Management applications, inside customers' tenants, began giving themselves directory roles. Mostly Exchange Administrator, in about fifty tenants. A few hours later, in one tenant, the Teams application made itself Global Administrator. Then a Python script added new secrets, valid until twenty twenty eight, to two other applications in that tenant. One belonged to the customer's managed service provider. The other belonged to Huntress, the security company.

Think about that last move for a moment. The attacker didn't just break in. It quietly cut itself a spare key to the security company's own application, inside the victim's house. So that when the ConnectWise door was eventually locked, it would still have a way in. That is the move of someone who has done this before.

Then, through the morning of the fifteenth, tokens were collected across tenants, in neat sorted order. Then a targeted sweep of sixteen organisations working on policy, national security, trade and critical minerals. Then, in the afternoon, what looks like a human taking over for a second, more careful pass through Exchange, SharePoint, Teams and Azure. The machine does the volume. The person does the judgement. That division of labour is exactly what Microsoft describes, and it's what you should expect from now on.

ConnectWise disabled the apps on the seventeenth, finished resetting credentials on the twentieth, and began restoring service yesterday.

How do you look for it?

First, find the ConnectWise and SkyKick applications in every tenant you have. Search by application I D, not by name, because names can be changed and unused integrations are easily forgotten. The I Ds are all in the brief.

Second, check the sign in logs for those applications. Not user sign ins. Service principal sign ins, which many teams never look at. Go back to the first of October, focus on the fifteenth and sixteenth, and flag anything from addresses that aren't Microsoft's. The brief lists the known ones.

Third, the uncomfortable part. Look at the Graph activity logs. Microsoft notes that if you find nothing, that doesn't prove nothing happened, because reading the directory leaves no audit record. Absence of evidence, in this case, is mostly absence of logging.

Fourth, and most important, look in the audit logs for any application that added itself to a role, and for any new secret or certificate added to any application since the thirteenth of October. Not just ConnectWise applications. Every application. Especially those belonging to your managed service providers, your backup tools and your security tools. And look specifically for secrets set to expire in twenty twenty eight. Those are the spare keys.

Fifth, look for mailbox access by applications soon after a role was granted, and for new forwarding or inbox rules.

And because the spraying is machine speed, alert on bursts of account lockouts coming from hosting providers. A person can't try two thousand nine hundred passwords a minute. A tool can.

If you find it. Disable the affected applications, but don't delete them. Deleting destroys the consent records and the evidence. Keep them disabled until ConnectWise tells you otherwise. Preserve the logs. Then remove any secret, certificate, role or permission you can't account for, and rotate the credentials for the affected applications.

And then the harder work, which is policy. Restrict who and what can add secrets to applications, and how long those secrets live. Keep the number of people with application administrator roles small. Apply Conditional Access to applications, not only to people. And review every third party application that holds the power to read all mail, or to change directory roles. Most firms have more of those than they realise. Most were consented once, by someone who has since left.

Why does this matter to us?

Because it combines the three things I've talked about most this month. A trusted supplier's application, holding high privilege in our clients' tenants and perhaps our own. A state actor with an interest in law, policy, trade and critical minerals, which is to say in the advice our clients pay for. And an A I framework that lets a small team operate at the scale of a large one, around the clock.

The old picture of espionage was a patient man in a hotel bar. The new one is a patient man with a machine doing the bar crawl for him, in two thousand places at once, and calling him over only when someone looks interested.

The defence hasn't changed as much as the attack. Know what has access. Know who granted it. Notice when it changes. It's unglamorous. It's also exactly what Storm-0940 is counting on you not to do.
