## Breaches and confirmed incidents

Let us start with the Korean story, because it has the quality of a parable.

The Financial Security Institute is the financial sector's own security body. Around three thousand financial firms send it material for their regular security assessments, through an online portal. Someone found a flaw in that portal and used it to create a fake account with the privileges of the Financial Supervisory Service, the regulator. The Institute says the regulator's real credentials were not leaked. What was taken: about two hundred administrative forms, about three hundred personal data entries, mostly names and dates of birth, and about three hundred and fifty records of system users, with names and phone numbers masked. Some smaller firms had submitted documents without masking, so some of it may be in plain text. The Institute says no system diagrams, security information or resident registration numbers were taken. The Financial Services Commission has started an on site investigation. There is an unconfirmed industry claim that the attacker came in through a temporary page used when text message authentication fails. That would fit. The fallback page is always the one nobody tested. The broader point is that the place where regulated firms send their security evidence is, by definition, a catalogue of how each of them is defended.

Japan. The Japan Chamber of Commerce and Industry runs online exams, bookkeeping among them, on a system hosted by a contractor. A malicious file was placed on it on the first of November. Up to about nine hundred and eighty thousand examinees may be affected, covering records back to April twenty twenty two. Names, dates of birth, scores, and contact details where people entered them. The route in has not been disclosed.

Two older Japanese incidents only now reported widely. Hana Cupid, the flower delivery service, with up to four point two four million records of login identifiers, hashed passwords and email addresses, from mid October. And H two O Retailing, which owns the Hankyu and Hanshin department stores, with about one point two six million member records, reached through a department store reservation site.

Two online shops with the sort of detail that tells you how. SOFT ninety nine, the car care products company, let customers attach images to inquiry forms. Someone attached a program instead, which allowed remote commands and the reading of configuration files. Up to about a hundred and thirty seven thousand people, including the last four digits of cards, expiry dates and cardholder names. And Edion, the electronics retailer, had malicious code running in its payment page from the fourth of November, capturing up to five hundred and fifty six cards with security codes, plus login details for over a thousand people and more than five thousand gift card numbers with their PINs. The site was suspended on the seventh. An image upload field and a payment page. The two oldest tricks on the web, still paying their rent.

The e-ticketing service teket had its member system breached and is still down. And the Arara email delivery saga continues. Chiba Kogyo Bank lost a hundred and seventy one thousand customer email addresses through it. Arara's third report confirms just over eighty one million unique addresses, about three hundred and eighty client companies, and about seven point one million phishing emails sent through its own service at the end of October. It remains suspended.

Travel. The Amadeus booking data breach keeps growing. Finnair says about a hundred and sixteen thousand bookings were involved, and has reissued booking references. An aviation site's running total across airlines is now at least three hundred and forty four thousand bookings, including Japan Airlines with ninety two thousand, Cathay Pacific with fifty three thousand, Singapore Airlines with forty six thousand, and A N A with nearly thirty eight thousand. Korean Air and Condor have confirmed without numbers. The exposure ran from August twenty twenty three to September of this year. If your travel desk books through any of these carriers, and it does, assume your travellers' itineraries were readable for three years.

Notion has become a downstream victim of the Fireworks A I breach. Transcripts of Notion A I chats, processed for a feature called Insights, were exposed for fewer than one percent of workspaces. Notion says most transcripts could not be tied to a user, but their content could still identify people or companies. I would think carefully about what your colleagues have typed into Notion A I.

Briefly. The Philippine Bureau of Internal Revenue says some taxpayer profile information may have been exposed, while a group calling itself Happy Go Lucky claims more than ten million taxpayers, unconfirmed. Vanderbilt University restored most services yesterday, and the claim of a million Social Security numbers remains unverified. And no new material incident filings with the S E C.

## Exploited vulnerabilities

C I S A's catalogue now stands at one thousand seven hundred and seventy nine entries, six added yesterday.

The urgent pair is Barracuda. Two flaws in the Email Security Gateway chain together. One lets an attacker bypass authentication by importing a crafted configuration backup. The other runs commands as root through a crafted time zone value. The second also affects several other Barracuda appliances. Barracuda disclosed them on the second of November and has since updated its advisory to say both are being exploited against a small number of organisations. C I S A set a deadline of Friday, the thirteenth. Mandiant's disclosures are cited. Nobody has named an attacker. I would patch today and look at the gateway's logs while you are there.

The other four additions are due on the twenty fourth. Outlook, which I will come to. Chrome, from Monday. Cisco BroadWorks, an XML flaw that leaks configuration, fixed in August. And Openwave's Webtop messaging.

Microsoft's Patch Tuesday. The totals disagree, which is becoming a monthly tradition. Tenable counts one thousand and ninety three, SecurityWeek and C S O say one thousand and ninety four, and Bleeping Computer's headline says one thousand two hundred and thirty five, though I could not read the article. Sixty three are critical. The one that matters most is the Outlook zero day. Opening a crafted message or Office file makes Outlook connect to an attacker's server without the usual warning about external content, and in doing so it hands over the user's N T L M version two hash. The preview pane is not a way in, so somebody has to open the thing. It is rated only moderate. In practice, a captured hash can be cracked or relayed, and an attacker sending it to a law firm or a consultancy is not doing so for the pleasure of the correspondence. It affects Microsoft three six five Apps and Office L T S C twenty twenty one and twenty twenty four. Credit is disputed in an interesting way. SecurityWeek credits two researchers and notes one acknowledgement mentions Claude. Tenable says a researcher who used Claude, and C S O says an Anthropic researcher using Claude.

Other Microsoft fixes worth a look. An Active Directory Domain Services remote code execution flaw. An Exchange remote code execution flaw, rated eight point eight by Tenable and nine point eight by C S O, which says it can be triggered by email. An unauthenticated L D A P remote code execution flaw at nine point eight. And a SharePoint privilege escalation at nine point nine that Microsoft, with characteristic restraint, calls important. This is also the final update for Windows eleven twenty three H two Enterprise and Education.

Fortinet. Eight advisories, seven rated nine or higher. The headline is FortiManager and FortiAnalyzer, where a JSON R P C interface allows unauthenticated code execution at nine point eight, though that interface is off by default. There is also SQL injection through FortiManager's O F T P protocol leading to code execution, an authentication bypass in FortiOS's O S P F routing, and a missing authentication check in FortiOS high availability that allows super admin commands, for which the seven point six point eight fix is still upcoming and a virtual patch is offered instead. None is reported exploited. But Fortinet's own scoring indicates proof of concept or working exploit code exists for some, and the details are now public. Most of these were found internally with help from Claude Mythos. The mitigations are specific. Set R P C permit to none, and keep the O F T P and F G F M management ports off the internet. If you have FortiManager facing the internet, this is your Thursday problem, and I would rather it were your Wednesday one.

SAP. Forty two notes, including what Onapsis calls ICM two Shell, memory corruption in how NetWeaver's internet communication manager and Web Dispatcher reassemble WebSocket messages, allowing unauthenticated commands as the S A P administrator. Onapsis gave SAP a working exploit. Recall Monday's finding that the median time from an SAP note to a working exploit is now one to three days. Adobe fixed about a hundred and ten flaws, with six perfect tens in Adobe Connect.

Three more. Xen Orchestra, the management tool for XCP N G virtualisation, has an authentication bypass using a crafted cookie, being actively exploited, with no C V E yet; the brief lists the indicators, including a virtual machine named pivot two. Huntress described zero days in the AhsayCBS backup server exploited in October, with attackers stealing Veeam credentials. And Git fixed a flaw that lets a malicious repository run code during a recursive clone, which is worth knowing for anyone whose build systems clone repositories they did not write.

## A I attacks and trends

NEAR A I, which runs confidential A I inference inside trusted execution environments, was breached through a remote code execution flaw in SGLang, the open source inference engine. The attacker could see that day's requests, including ones in confidential mode, then moved sideways to steal account details for about a hundred users and chat histories and logs from eighteen agents. Two customers' A P I keys were exposed and revoked. NEAR A I's own summary is admirably honest. A trusted execution environment, it says, does not protect against a vulnerability in the software running inside the environment. The vault is only as good as whatever you put in it with the money.

Anthropic released Claude Mythos five point two, limited to vetted organisations, and Fable five point two. Mythos scored about ninety percent on a benchmark of rediscovering known vulnerabilities, and, in more than eighteen hundred test scenarios, used credentials planted outside its sandbox three point one percent of the time, against twenty one percent for the previous version. Fable five point two completed about seventy six percent of an independent multi stage attack benchmark, against sixty one percent for OpenAI's G P T six Cyber. Google opened its Gemini four Carbon security model to paying customers, and says Wiz used it to find zero days at two major software as a service providers within two hours.

Put those next to this week's patches. The exploited Outlook zero day was found with Claude, and most of Fortinet's eight critical flaws were found with Claude Mythos. The defenders' tools are now finding the bugs that end up in our patch cycle. The same capability, as we have discussed, is available to the other side. The window between disclosure and exploitation is closing from both ends.

Briefly, an older item. Google's threat intelligence team described attackers stealing access to A I models and running up charges above a hundred thousand dollars a day at some victims, a practice called L L M jacking. No new deepfake incidents.

Russia. ESET published research on a Turla toolkit it calls Cereal, which lives inside Microsoft Exchange servers. One component injects into the Exchange transport service and collects messages with document or P D F attachments as they pass through. There is no external command server. The compromised Exchange servers themselves are the command hub, listening on web addresses that look like normal Exchange traffic. Victims were a Romanian government body, where it was set to collect mail involving N A T O and European Union addresses, and a Kazakh government body. ESET attributes it to Turla, the group linked to Russia's F S B, with high confidence. If you still run Exchange on your own servers, the brief lists the file artefacts to look for.

## Deep dive: the quartermaster

Today's deep dive comes from Check Point Research, published yesterday after what it describes as a year long investigation. It is about a supplier rather than an operator, and it is a useful corrective to the way we usually talk about Chinese espionage groups.

Check Point tracks a developer it calls CopperDragon. In its words, CopperDragon may serve as a digital quartermaster for several China nexus actors. The customers include FamousSparrow, Earth Estries, TropicTrooper and a group tracked as U A T ninety two forty four. The shared product line is the SparrowDoor family of backdoors and what Check Point calls the Sparrow framework. Check Point also assesses that CopperDragon maintains a Linux malware family called Melofee, based on code and infrastructure overlaps.

Now, the caveats, and Check Point is careful with them. It says plainly that shared tooling or shared command servers do not necessarily mean two intrusions were run by the same actor. The link to the third campaign is weaker than the others, resting on shared infrastructure and on ESET's observation that one backdoor replaced another on the same machines, with no direct code similarity. And while one outlet mentions a possible overlap between FamousSparrow and Salt Typhoon, the telecom intruders, it calls the link unclear. So, reasonable confidence that one developer is behind the tools. Much less confidence that one team is behind the operations.

There are three campaigns.

The first, which Check Point calls SilentDelivery, has been tracked since early this year across the Czech Republic, Germany, France, Taiwan, Nepal, Hong Kong and Indonesia. It uses watering holes. Government, shipping and I T or research websites were compromised, and selectively serve visitors a piece of obfuscated JavaScript that shows a fake Chrome certificate error. The page offers a helpful certificate fix, an installer called CertFixer. People who install it receive a new backdoor called SquadC, or a SparrowDoor variant, which persists through a registry run key called Windows Update Assist. One of the websites was compromised through cross site scripting. In Indonesia there was also ordinary phishing, a shortcut file disguised as a P D F inside an archive. The lesson is old but still unlearned. A browser certificate error that offers you a download is not a certificate error.

The second, OceanGate, mainly in Asia against government and critical infrastructure, starts on an internet facing Linux server. From there the operators guess passwords over remote desktop against Windows machines until an administrator account works. On Linux they plant a Go backdoor, trojanised versions of the S S H client and server, and tunnelling tools. On Windows, a keylogger and tools to dump credentials. And SparrowDoor uses a packet capture driver to listen passively on port four four three, so it never needs to open a port of its own, while a modified rootkit hides the evidence.

The third, DarkWire, from mid twenty twenty five, targets at least three major Latin American telecoms, plus foreign affairs and justice bodies. It uses a loader, a backdoor, a tool that tunnels data inside D N S mail exchanger queries, a proxy disguised as the penetration testing tool sqlmap, and the SoftEther V P N renamed to look like a VMware service. It moves laterally over S M B and Windows Management Instrumentation, and loads its malicious libraries alongside legitimate signed programs, including V L C. Telecoms, as we have said before, are rarely the final target. They are the road to everyone who uses them.

What to hunt for. The written brief carries the domains, addresses, certificate hashes and about twenty file hashes that Check Point published, but the behavioural artefacts are more durable. The registry run key Windows Update Assist, and any installer named CertFixer. A local account named admin with a dollar sign, added to administrators. Firewall rules opening port four four five inbound with odd names. The WinDivert packet driver on servers that have no business running it. A keylogger window titled k b l. A copy of sqlmap outside your red team's tooling. Trojanised S S H binaries on Linux, which you can catch by checking them against the package manager. Signed programs such as V L C running from Windows system folders or program data vendor folders, where they do not belong. A VMware tools process on a machine that is not a VMware guest. Bursts of D N S mail exchanger queries. And T L S on port sixty five thirty two to the Vultr hosting network. Check Point published no ATT and CK mapping, so the one in the brief is our inference, marked as such.

Why this matters to us. It is tempting to treat each named Chinese group as a separate opponent with a separate playbook. The quartermaster model says otherwise. Several groups draw from the same armoury, so detections built on one group's tooling will often fire on another's, and a backdoor you find may tell you less about who is inside than you would like. For a firm with offices in many of the countries on this list, and staff who visit government and research websites as a matter of routine, the watering hole campaign is the one to take personally. The fake certificate fix is aimed at exactly the kind of busy, competent person who wants the problem to go away.
