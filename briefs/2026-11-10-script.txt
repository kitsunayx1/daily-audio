## Morning Signal. Tuesday, the tenth of November.

Good morning. Tuesday. For a month, the people who switched off Europe's supermarket freezers were a rumour with a Telegram channel. Yesterday, France gave them names, ranks and an employer. The employer is Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Meanwhile a company whose software runs a great many other companies' computers told its customers to switch theirs off, and a researcher showed what happens when a trusted vendor's keys end up in the wrong pocket.

This is Morning Signal for Tuesday, the tenth of November. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about five yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief carries every source link, and the organisations table has eighty eight rows.

Two housekeeping notes. Microsoft's Patch Tuesday and Fortinet's promised disclosures had not been published when this was put together, so you will hear about them tomorrow. And Reddit and Mastodon were unreachable again, so the social section is partial.

Three things.

## The top three

Number one. France has formally attributed the October sabotage of supermarket refrigeration to a unit of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps called Shahid Kaveh, which American agencies regard as the same outfit as Cyber Avengers. About seven hundred French grocery stores were hit. Three Guard officers are named, the Paris prosecutor wants international arrest warrants, and France will ask for European sanctions. Number two. Two vendors with keys to many customers' houses had a very bad day. N able took its hosted N central service offline and told customers running it themselves to shut it down, after a researcher published thirty three critical flaws together with working exploits. Nobody has reported exploitation yet. And Wiz described an attacker who used the cloud data platform Qubole's own Amazon account to step into its customers' accounts and help himself to their secrets. That is today's deep dive.

Number three. C I S A added four flaws to its exploited list yesterday, with a deadline of this Thursday, among them the OpenSSH bug called SSHole. Chrome fixed its eighth exploited zero day of the year. And Silent Ransom Group added another law firm, Wilentz, Goldman and Spitzer.

## Breaches and confirmed incidents

Japan first, because Japan is still where the counting happens.

Sharefull, a gig work matching app, says someone got into its internal management system on the evening of the fourth of November, Tokyo time. It noticed the next morning, closed the route that afternoon, and told the public yesterday. The data possibly exposed covers up to about seven and a half million individual accounts and about seven thousand five hundred client companies. And it is not light data. Names, phone numbers, email addresses, home addresses, dates of birth, bank details used for paying wages, links to face photographs, emergency contacts, and information about residence status. In other words, everything you would need to impersonate a casual worker, or to find one. The cause and the culprit are not disclosed.

Wills, which runs the NetMile points exchange, says up to about five point four nine million member records may be exposed, including members who left. Rohto Pharmaceutical disclosed that someone used valid login details for its mail order call centre phone system to reach several customer systems in September, and that data on two million, seven hundred and ninety seven thousand, five hundred and forty five people leaked. Some of the call recordings contained card details, which is a reminder that a call centre recording is a database nobody remembers to protect. Iris Ohyama, the manufacturer and retailer, is older news, disclosed at the end of October, but the figure has only now been widely reported. About seven point seven million people, from a cloud service its online store used. Doutor Coffee had its online shop's admin panel opened in late October, about twenty five thousand members. And, with a certain irony, the Japan Network Security Association's security literacy checking service lost up to fifteen thousand user records.

MUJI's parent, Ryohin Keikaku, issued its fourth report. It now describes credential stuffing against its online store, with about two hundred and seventy thousand accounts possibly logged into using passwords stolen elsewhere. The count of records with names, addresses and phone numbers went from about five hundred and twenty thousand to about seven hundred and fifty thousand.

Now the email delivery story, which has become something larger than Arara. Gakken, the education publisher, says seven hundred and ninety three thousand records leaked across seventeen group companies through Arara. SEGA says two point two million newsletter subscriber addresses were exposed, and stresses this is unrelated to an earlier incident at its arcade network. Bleeping Computer also lists MOS Burger with seven and a half million addresses, a cinema chain and an internet provider. And the Japanese researcher who writes as piyolog has put together a roundup showing four more email delivery providers compromised in the same period. Blastmail, Cuenote, SpeeCAN RAIDEN and Y mail. One of them output more than fifty one million addresses, cumulatively. Another had a path traversal flaw that leaked a secret key, which was then used to send about one point four million phishing emails. This is no longer one vendor's bad month. It is a whole layer of Japanese marketing infrastructure, the companies that send newsletters on behalf of brands, falling over at once. Anyone who received a Japanese brand's email in October should treat its next one with suspicion.

South Korea. Hanil Networks, which runs I T infrastructure for card companies, disclosed a ransomware attack from the second of November, a week late, and says it found no data theft. The Douzone Bizon backup breach now has twenty nine suspected victims, including NH NongHyup Bank's A I subsidiary, Agile Soda. And Korea's privacy regulator confirmed that Anthropic reported, at the end of October, that contact details for about a hundred Korean corporate Claude accounts were exposed. The cause was a security firm, during a bug bounty engagement, getting into third party business software Anthropic uses. Korean and American reports spell the firm's name differently. Anthropic says no source code or model weights were touched. A parking app, Modu Parking, had its messaging vendor breached and its sending keys used to phish its own customers in its own name. When a vendor can speak for you, a stolen vendor key is a stolen voice.

Thai Airways says Amadeus, the airline booking system provider, told it of unauthorised access to booking data inside Amadeus's own infrastructure, running from August twenty twenty three to September of this year. Just over sixty thousand booking records, and three hundred and seventy six passengers with passport numbers and contact details. No card data. Three years is a long time to be in somebody's booking system, and Amadeus serves a great many airlines.

Elsewhere. Vanderbilt University declared an active cybersecurity incident yesterday and told staff and students to shut down their university devices. A group called BYOD claims more than a million Social Security numbers and control of the domain. None of that is verified, and Vanderbilt's health system says it is not affected. The Philippine Securities and Exchange Commission issued an advisory about a possible breach including tax identification numbers. And a Russian hosting company, ESTT, had its cloud encrypted on Sunday and has restored most customer machines.

## Exploited vulnerabilities

C I S A's catalogue was updated yesterday evening and stands at one thousand seven hundred and seventy three entries. Four additions, all due this Thursday, the twelfth.

GPUStack, an open source server for running A I models. Its loopback check is off by default, so a request that appears to come from the machine itself, or one with a forged header claiming to, gets administrator rights. Behind a reverse proxy on the same host, that is everyone. Attackers used it to run reverse shells on a live deployment. Upgrade to two point two point two or later, and in the meantime turn on the localhost check and strip that header at the proxy. If your data science teams run their own model servers, and they do, ask them.

OpenSSH SSHole is now in the catalogue. GreyNoise has watched post exploitation from six addresses across eighteen attack chains, with attackers querying cloud metadata, reading password hashes and root keys, and adding their own authorized keys. In one case they installed a Python command and control agent. watchTowr says only botnets and cryptominers have adopted it so far. That will not last. Alpine, Buildroot, OpenWrt and Yocto builds without P A M are the ones in scope. Patch to ten point six p two, or turn off password authentication.

The other two are FreeSWITCH, the telephony platform, and I B M Aspera Faspex, a managed file transfer product. I would underline Aspera. Managed file transfer products have been targeted again and again for mass data theft.

Chrome. Google fixed an exploited use after free in its X M L handling yesterday, the eighth Chrome zero day of the year. Version one five seven point zero point eight one zero three point fifty or fifty one. Edge, Brave and the other Chromium browsers will follow. Google says only that an exploit exists in the wild.

N able. A researcher named Sean Bales published more than thirty flaws in N central, the remote monitoring and management platform used by managed service providers, together with exploit code. The published chains go from stealing credentials anonymously, to creating an administrator account, to running code as root. N able calls the disclosure non responsible, which is true and also beside the point. It took the hosted service offline in the Americas, Asia Pacific and Europe, and told customers who run it on their own servers to shut them down until further notice. It says it has seen no exploitation. One source says a hotfix is expected around midday Eastern today, another gives no date. Here is why it matters to us. N central is how many outsourced I T providers reach into their clients' machines. If any of your suppliers, or any of the smaller firms you work with, are managed by a provider using it, their exposure is now yours. An earlier N central flaw was already on C I S A's exploited list. Public exploit code for a management platform is an invitation that tends to be accepted.

SonicWall's multi factor bypass is still due on the twentieth. The bypass needs valid credentials for an alternate login method, so this is a password plus problem, not a no password problem. The same release fixes two management interface flaws and a pre authentication denial of service.

And today. Patch Tuesday, around midday Eastern. Fortinet was due to publish the identifiers for the fixes it shipped in late October, across FortiOS, FortiManager, FortiAnalyzer, FortiMail and FortiAuthenticator. When we looked, its advisory page had not changed since the middle of October. watchTowr expects the fixes to be taken apart and exploited quickly once the details land, and advises patching now and taking exposed management interfaces off the internet. I would do both before lunch.

## A I attacks and trends

Mittwald, a German web host that serves web agencies, says an attacker got in through its Model Context Protocol server, the interface that lets customers connect A I assistants to its hosting platform. Fifty six customer projects were compromised, and the attacker went unnoticed for eight days. The managing director says the logs show only seconds between steps, which he takes as evidence the attacker was using A I. That is the only evidence offered, and fast is not the same as artificial. But the larger point stands regardless of who or what was typing. Every company that bolts an A I connector onto its platform has opened a new front door, and some of those doors are being tried.

GPUStack, which I just mentioned, belongs here too. A I infrastructure is now infrastructure, with the same exploited vulnerabilities as everything else.

Google DeepMind, with cryptographers from U C San Diego and Inria, used A I agents to make an attack on old R S A signing keys about three and a half times cheaper. It is a signature forgery attack against hardware security modules using ten twenty four bit keys, it needs about four billion signing queries in advance, and it does not recover the key. DeepMind is careful to say only obsolete ten twenty four bit systems are affected. Two agents built and debugged the computation in under four days. The practical action is modest. Find any ten twenty four bit keys still sitting in hardware security modules or legacy signing systems, and retire them. The interesting part is not the cryptography. It is that two agents and twenty human messages did a research project.

More detail on BOSSAI, the A I assisted hacking operation we covered last week. Acronis is highly confident it is one person, in Hualien City, Taiwan, identified through a reused GitHub profile, an S S H key name, an email address and a home internet address that appeared in a conversation with Grok. He ran twelve agents, mostly on stripped down local models, redirected Claude Code and Codex to one of them, and targeted hundreds of organisations in eleven countries. About ten had data accessed. More than half of his scanner's serious findings were false positives, and it only worked against weak defences. So, one man, a dozen agents, and an operational security failure involving a chatbot. The future of crime, it turns out, still leaves fingerprints.

Two older items surfaced in research roundups. Zenity showed that a single prompt to an exposed agent on Amazon's Bedrock AgentCore could reach the instance metadata service and yield credentials to take over every agent in the same account and region. Amazon has patched it. And Microsoft patched a Visual Studio Copilot flaw where naming a file with a trailing dot got around Copilot's refusal to edit its own configuration.

And Fireworks A I, the model hosting platform, disclosed a second breach, this time through a third party cloud provider, exposing billing data and, for some customers, partial model weights and datasets. It links the activity to people stealing access to A I compute and reselling it.

## Cyber geopolitics

The French attribution is the most significant public attribution of a disruptive attack in Europe in some time, so let me give it its due.

On Monday, France's cyber crisis coordination centre published a five page joint report from ANSSI, the military's cyber command, and both intelligence services, the D G S E and the D G S I. It says the I R G C's Shahid Kaveh unit sabotaged refrigeration systems at about seven hundred French grocery stores in October. The Record reports food losses and at least fifteen fires. The evidence cited is Farsi language artefacts, tooling, and infrastructure also used to scout other industrial equipment. ANSSI separately tied Iranian hackers to attacks on Schneider Electric equipment at small hydroelectric sites. An annex names three men, the head of the Guard's electronic and cyber centre, Hamid Reza Lashgarian, and two Shahid Kaveh commanders, Mahdi Lashgarian and Reza Salarvand. Salarvand had not been publicly named before. The Paris prosecutor has opened a judicial investigation and asked for international arrest warrants. The foreign minister, Jean Noël Barrot, announced it at the G seven meeting in Paris, said France would seek E U sanctions, and added that France might respond with measures, quote, including some that we will not discuss publicly. The Iranian ambassador was summoned this morning. Kaja Kallas, the E U's foreign policy chief, said the response should be strong. Iran has said nothing.

Now the caveats. SecurityWeek dates the attack to the twelfth of October, describes store temperatures reaching fifty degrees for about six hours, reports one chain losing tens of millions of euros in a day, and does not mention fires. More importantly, the French report does not cover the second wave in early November, which reached at least eleven European countries, the French blood service and British meat suppliers. That wave remains unattributed, and was claimed by pro Russian hacktivists, unverified. So we may be looking at an Iranian operation followed by copycats, or something more coordinated. France has not said.

What I take from it. When a government names individual officers, asks for arrest warrants and hints at a response it will not describe, it is telling the other side it can see them. Anyone with industrial controllers on the internet, building management included, should assume the attention will continue.

The NoName zero five seven sixteen target list. The Record and Il Sole Ventiquattro Ore have the full eighteen names now, and it is not just politicians. The president of Eurojust, the head of Europol's cybercrime centre, the president of Germany's federal police, Rome's chief prosecutor, the head of Italy's postal police, the mayor of Aviano, and three German journalists who investigated Russian hackers. The group pays cryptocurrency for further personal information about the people on it, and their relatives, through a portal it calls the Tribunal. It looks like retaliation for last year's Europol takedown. A bounty for personal data on named people who annoyed you is a technique, and techniques travel.

Briefly. Ahead of Spain's election on the twenty ninth, an open source research firm describes pro Russian Telegram amplification, fake news pages run from Vietnam, A I generated images of destroyed ballots, and a leak of data on about two thousand security personnel. The U S Treasury's financial crimes unit has moved to cut the crypto exchange MEXC off from the American financial system, citing at least sixteen point seven billion dollars in illicit flows linked to Russia, Iran and North Korea. Nvidia's board is investigating reports that a chip buyer was gathering information on its chief executive, Jensen Huang, for Beijing. No system access is alleged, and China calls it a smear. Fortinet describes a new backdoor, PapRAT, from the South Asian espionage group Bitter. And Thailand's National Security Council has ordered preparations for a national cyber emergency.

No change on Binance, Rosstat, or the expiring information sharing law in Washington.

## Deep dive: the vendor's keys

Today's deep dive is fresh. Wiz published it yesterday, with The Hacker News following, and it describes something every firm that buys software as a service should think about this morning.

Qubole is a data lake platform, owned by Idera. Like many such services, it works by having customers create a role in their own Amazon Web Services account that Qubole's account is allowed to step into. The customer says, in effect, this outside account may act as this role, provided it presents an agreed External I D. That is how the platform reaches your data to process it. It is entirely normal, and it is how a great many analytics, monitoring and security vendors work.

Between the fourteenth and the twenty sixth of October, someone used credentials belonging to Qubole's own platform account to do exactly that, into multiple customers' accounts. Every request carried a valid External I D, and every request succeeded. Wiz makes a point I would like you to remember. External I Ds are not secrets. They exist to stop one customer being confused with another, not to stop the vendor's own account being used by a stranger. Most of the roles abused were not even in use any more. The customers had stopped using Qubole, or never really started, but the trust was still there.

The attacker is unattributed. Wiz calls it opportunistic, The Hacker News says the targeting does not look like a state group, and how the attacker obtained Qubole's credentials has not been disclosed. A data security firm called Sentra first noticed the activity in its customers' environments and alerted Wiz. Neither Qubole nor Idera had said anything when this was published.

The timeline. On the fourteenth of October, the first role assumptions, from a Mullvad V P N address. The next day most of the work moves to a server at the hosting company O V H. On the eighteenth, a second identity in Qubole's account is used, and another Mullvad address joins in. The last activity from the main server is on the twenty sixth. There was also a separate burst between the eighth and eleventh of October using customers' own access keys, apparently created for Qubole integrations, all tested within one hour. Wiz could not link that to the rest.

What the attacker did once inside is a tidy lesson in where the valuable things are kept. First, a broad look around. Storage buckets, databases, compute instances, networks, roles, encryption keys. Then straight for the places where people leave credentials. The environment variables of Lambda functions. Secrets Manager. The Systems Manager parameter store. And then the attacker tested what it found, using TruffleHog, the open source secret scanner. Amazon keys were checked against Amazon. Entra I D client secrets were tried against Microsoft. Okta client credentials were tried against Okta. That last step is the one that should worry you. A breach that starts in a vendor's cloud account and ends with working credentials for your identity provider is no longer a cloud breach. It is a breach of everything your identity provider protects. Finally, data. Object listings and downloads from storage, sometimes entire buckets in one session.

Wiz also found a staged implant that it did not see deployed. A bootable Linux disk image called sso agent dot iso, which reports system information to the O V H server, waits for shell scripts on a high port, and reboots itself after ninety minutes. The name is a nice touch. Nobody looks twice at something called S S O agent.

So what to do. Wiz's hunting guidance is specific, and the indicators are all in the written brief. Look in CloudTrail from the first of October for role assumptions from Qubole's account number where the source address is not an Amazon address. Look for the two principal identifiers Wiz published, remembering that one of them is also used by legitimate Qubole traffic, so it only matters when it comes from outside Amazon. Look for session names beginning with q u i a m. Look for the TruffleHog user agent, both in CloudTrail and, importantly, in your Entra and Okta sign in logs. And for every suspicious session, review everything it touched in Secrets Manager, Systems Manager, Lambda and storage. Be aware that if you were not logging storage data events, you will not be able to tell which objects were read, and several victims could not.

The response steps are straightforward and unglamorous. Rotate every secret the role could read, and revoke active sessions, because rotation alone does not end a session already issued. Delete vendor roles you no longer use. Replace trust in a vendor's entire account with trust in specific vendor identities, and add conditions limiting where the requests may come from. Rotate External I Ds, and use a different one per role. And turn on organisation wide CloudTrail, with data events on the buckets that matter.

I will add one hunt of my own, and the brief marks it as mine. Do not stop at Qubole. Alert on any vendor account assuming a role in your environment from a V P N or a commodity hosting provider. Legitimate vendors usually come from their own cloud address space. A vendor arriving through Mullvad is not a vendor.

The broader point. We spend our energy deciding whether to trust a vendor at the moment we sign the contract. We spend very little deciding when to stop. The roles in this story were mostly abandoned, still trusting a company their owners had moved on from, and still holding the keys to secrets that led all the way to the identity provider. Somewhere in most cloud estates, including large consultancies and their client engagement accounts, there is a role like that. Today would be a good day to find it.
