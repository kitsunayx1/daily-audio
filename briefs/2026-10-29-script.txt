## Morning Signal. Thursday, the twenty ninth of October.

Good morning. Thursday. Two more big law firms have appeared on an extortion site, Palo Alto firewalls are being opened with a P H P bug, and somewhere in a satellite company's network, a router has been quietly copying down the keys to every branch office it talks to.

This is Morning Signal for Thursday, the twenty ninth of October. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about five yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief carries every source link, and the organisations table has seventy five rows for your vendor and client lookups. A busy day for the table.

Three things first.

## Supply chain

Supply chain.

Victims are lured with DocuSign or proposal themed messages, or tax and Teams themes, into enrolling their computers into a device management service. Hexnode or Rippling. ## Deep dive: the keys to the branch offices

Now the deep dive. On Wednesday, Cisco Talos published research on a group it tracks as U A T eleven four seven three, and a toolkit it calls KeyDrift. It's the most technically elegant thing I've read this month, and that is not a compliment to anyone's network.

Let me set the scene. Most large organisations now connect their offices through software defined wide area networks. S D W A N. At the centre sit controllers. Cisco calls them vSmart and vBond. They decide how traffic flows between sites, and they hand out the encryption keys that protect the tunnels between offices. If an ordinary router is a postman, the controller is the sorting office, the address book and the key cabinet.

U A T eleven four seven three got into those controllers through a Cisco flaw from February, rated a perfect ten. It's been on C I S A's exploited list since then. Talos doesn't say when these intrusions happened, or whether the flaw was still a zero day when it was used. It calls the activity historical.

Once inside, they didn't drop a crude backdoor and leave. They moved into the furniture. KeyDrift lives inside the controller's own control plane process, called v daemon. The main module, which Talos calls PolyFlame, is injected into that process using the debugging interface, by a small injector and a shell script that finds the right process. PolyFlame hooks the functions the process uses to send and receive network traffic, and gives the operators a backdoor and a relay. On vBond, a packed version arrives encrypted, makes its own system calls to avoid monitoring, and quits if it notices a debugger watching.

Then the module that gives the toolkit its name. On vSmart, a library Talos calls LittleFilch hooks the point where the controller decrypts its own encrypted control traffic. It reads the decrypted messages and pulls out the peer addresses and, Talos assesses, the thirty two byte I P sec keys the controller distributes to the edges. It writes them, encrypted, to a file in a temporary folder, and sends them out over U D P, disguised as ordinary secure datagrams.

Think about what that means. With those keys, and a position on the network path, an attacker can potentially decrypt the traffic between your offices, or redirect it. Not one laptop. The whole overlay.

How do they persist? By editing the startup scripts for the controller's own services, so that every time S S H starts, their loader starts first, and by quietly removing some of the authentication configuration for logins. They timestomp their files, copying the dates from legitimate neighbours, so nothing looks freshly changed.

And how do they talk to it? There are no hardcoded addresses, no domains, no beacon you can block. The commands arrive inside the S D W A N control traffic itself. And the implant checks a hash of today's date against its own clock before obeying. A password that changes every midnight.

Who is it? Talos says with high confidence that the group's infrastructure overlaps considerably with Volt Typhoon's Silent Train network, the one Lumen described the same day. Talos stops short of saying they're the same group. The victims Talos names are in space and satellite services, defence and aerospace technology, and policy research. That last one should catch the eye of anyone who works near government advisory.

So, what to look for. All of this is in the brief, with the file paths and the Snort rule numbers, because Talos published no hashes or I P addresses.

One. Compare the startup scripts for the S S H and A A A manager services on your controllers with known good images, looking for a remount of the root filesystem, or anything launched before the real S S H.

Two. Look for unexpected shared libraries loaded into v daemon. Specifically, libdssl and libcommon.

Three. Look for anything attached to v daemon with the debugging interface, and for the files vdaemon dot bin or vdaemon dot conf in the temporary folder.

Four. Check for missing or altered login authentication files, and for files whose timestamps match a neighbour exactly.

Five. Review controller logs for signs of the February flaw. Unexpected peers, unexpected control connections, new admin accounts.

And six. Look at who manages your controllers, and from where.

What to do if you find it. First, take a memory capture and Cisco's diagnostic bundle before you reboot or upgrade anything, because reboots destroy the evidence. Then patch, and apply Cisco's hardening guide. If implants are present, bring in Cisco's support team and rebuild the controllers from known good images. Cleaning them isn't enough. And then the expensive part. Assume the overlay keys are compromised. Rotate keys and certificates across the overlay, and re onboard the edges. Finally, if your S D W A N is run for you by a managed service provider, which for many firms it is, ask them these questions directly, and ask for the answers in writing.

The lesson is an old one, dressed in new clothes. We encrypt everything, and we feel safe, and we forget that encryption only moves the problem to wherever the keys are kept. KeyDrift doesn't break the lock. It sits next to the locksmith and takes notes.
