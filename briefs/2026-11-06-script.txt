## Breaches and confirmed incidents

Chainalysis first, because a great many compliance, investigations and law enforcement teams use it. On Wednesday morning, Eastern, the company said an on path attack, which is to say someone sitting in the middle of a connection, had exposed a subset of customer passwords. Forty minutes later it took its K Y T transaction monitoring, address screening and login page offline after what it called abnormal activity. Then, at eleven on Wednesday night, Eastern, it said that names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all users had been exposed, and that some K Y T accounts appear to have been accessed without authorisation. It says there is no evidence its investigations data was accessed or its attribution data tampered with. Customers will get back in only after submitting their I P addresses for an allow list, resetting passwords and rotating A P I keys. The Record quotes an email to customers calling it an ongoing multifront cyberattack. On the thirtieth of October, the same company said there was no evidence that credentials or session tokens had been accessed. It is worth remembering that the first statement in any breach is usually the most optimistic one you will read. If anyone on your side uses Chainalysis, rotate the credentials and keys today.

Japan. The M S and A D insurers. NHK reports about four hundred and eighty thousand records leaked across the group: about thirty seven thousand at Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance, policy numbers, names, addresses, phone numbers and email, with no card or bank data, and about five thousand three hundred at Mitsui Sumitomo Aioi Life. Aioi Nissay Dowa has not given its own count; TBS says its data includes the last four digits of card numbers. The intrusion was detected on the evening of the second; several customer portals are still down, with no restart date. SMBC Consumer Finance, which guarantees consumer loans for partner banks, detected and blocked an intrusion into its guarantee system on the second, but not before data was sent out. Up to three hundred and twenty thousand, one hundred and eighteen people are affected, and the data is the kind fraudsters dream of: names, dates of birth, addresses, phones, email, driving licence numbers, employers, incomes and guarantee details. Docomo SMBC Net Bank alone counts up to a hundred and twenty two thousand, six hundred and thirty five. Tsukuba Bank has stopped taking new card applications.

NTT Docomo found unauthorised access to its online shop and its customer account service on the fourth, stopped the procedures in the early hours of the fifth, and confirmed a leak that evening. Up to about three hundred thousand customers, with names, addresses, dates of birth and mobile numbers. Whether card data or national I D numbers were involved is still under investigation. The cause is unknown.

And Sompo Japan says about sixty five thousand car insurance customers were affected through its supplier, Prestige Core Solutions, whose total now stands at up to about five and a half million records. The cause: weak access controls and credentials in one of the supplier's web applications. Sony Sonpo, ENEOS Charge Plus and K D D I are also caught in it.

Nomura Investor Relations has published a second report. About two thousand institutional investors and analysts had names, emails, phone numbers, companies and titles exposed, along with about two hundred and fifty former employees.

DigiCert, the certificate authority, has confirmed a breach. A migration in February 2025 left an internal system holding archived customer data reachable from the internet, with a self registration feature and no multi factor authentication. An outside account read it between the twenty seventh of June and the second of July this year. Mostly business contact details, but a small minority of accounts also held passports, driving licences or utility bills, the documents people upload to prove who they are. DigiCert says its certificate services were not affected. A company whose product is trust, undone by a sign up form.

Crédit Mutuel Arkéa, a follow up. The service provider is still not named. The investigator ZachXBT now says more than a hundred and thirty three million dollars was moved, through stablecoins, bridges and finally Monero, that about thirty million could not be frozen because the bank's communication came too late, and that he has high confidence this was not North Korea's Lazarus Group. The bank still confirms only the order of magnitude.

Norway's aid agency, Norad, says attackers used twenty seven compromised accounts to get into its grants portal. The names and emails of all twenty thousand, six hundred and fourteen users, from more than twelve thousand organisations, may have been accessed, and applications were stolen from three organisations outside Norway. Norad says it cannot rule out exposure of applicants working in conflict zones and authoritarian states. A list of who is asking a Western donor for money, in places where that is dangerous, is intelligence in its purest form.

## Exploited vulnerabilities

No additions to C I S A's known exploited list in the last two days; the catalogue stays at one thousand seven hundred and sixty eight. The WordPress deadline for federal agencies is today. The Node two Shell flaw, in the Brotli compression library, is due on Tuesday, and VMware's S D D C Manager still shows patch pending. Cisco's advisory on that same flaw now lists nineteen affected Cisco products, with dozens more under investigation and nearly a dozen Splunk products. And one conflict: Help Net Security says no exploitation in the wild is known, while C I S A's list says otherwise. When C I S A says exploited, I tend to believe C I S A.

C W P door. The Chinese security firm Qi An Xin's X Lab has found a backdoored version of the web server inside Control Web Panel, a free hosting control panel. The trojanised binary runs commands hidden in signed web request headers, which never appear in the access logs, it carefully restores file timestamps, and when found it had zero detections out of sixty three on VirusTotal. Installing it needs root; how the attackers got root is not known. It was found alongside Ebury, the long running Linux credential stealer. The hunting commands are in the brief.

OpenSSH has issued an emergency fix, version ten point six p two, for a critical password authentication bypass on Alpine Linux and other musl based systems running without P A M. Under a narrow set of conditions, a long enough password logs you into an account that has no password at all, and on Alpine's tiny cloud images that account has passwordless root. The workaround is to turn off password authentication, which most people should have done already. Remember to restart S S H after upgrading; the package does not do it for you.

Barracuda. The research firm watchTowr has demonstrated the full chain against the Email Security Gateway: restore a forged configuration backup without logging in, because the checksum secret is hard coded, then inject a command through the timezone setting, and you are root. watchTowr proved it by writing a file as root and has published a detection tool, not an exploit. The fix is version nine point four point oh point thirty or later, released on Monday. Not exploited yet.

Also: WatchGuard fixed three critical flaws in its AuthPoint authentication gateway, and says its Dimension product runs a vulnerable Ubuntu for the Linux T C P flaw with no patch yet. Ubuntu itself still has not released those kernel fixes from its proposed channel. And Docker fixed a flaw in its sandboxes that let an A I agent use a symbolic link to copy files from outside the sandbox. Next week brings Patch Tuesday, Chrome one fifty eight and Fortinet's disclosures, all on Tuesday.

## Brand and executive targeting

The Southern District of New York has charged three men, Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit, over a scheme it says stole about a hundred and twenty nine million dollars. The method, in three acts. First, an email from the chief executive to someone in accounting, asking for help with a confidential deal. Second, a phone or video call, with the chief executive's voice, and sometimes face, cloned by A I. Third, a hand off to an attorney at a large international law firm, who was of course nobody of the kind; Soall, prosecutors say, mostly played British lawyers, from a script titled Lawyer Call. Thousands of companies were targeted over more than eighteen months, including hospital systems, a Rhode Island bank, a British fintech that lost eleven point five eight million dollars, and a Massachusetts mutual fund. All three were arrested in Spain or the United Kingdom and are awaiting extradition. One caution: the Justice Department's own page gives both more than a hundred million and a hundred and twenty nine million, and the named victims add up to about thirty two million.

If the firm's executives can be cloned, and they can, the defence is procedural: a call back on a known number, a second approver, and a culture where a junior person is thanked for refusing a chief executive.

## Deep dive: agents in the tenant

Today's deep dive is Microsoft Threat Intelligence's report, published just before midnight, U T C, on Thursday, titled Threat actors use agentic A I tooling to find and abuse exposed secrets.

Why it matters to us. Autonomous agents, running the A R T E X framework, search public GitHub for an organisation's leaked secrets, the client secrets and keys that applications use to log into the cloud, and turn them into real access: Entra I D, Azure, SharePoint, OneDrive, mail, Azure DevOps and Azure's A I services. Some victims were reached through a multitenant application owned by a vendor and installed across many customers. Microsoft specifically tells managed service providers to review their partner access. A firm with many client tenants, many vendor apps and many code repositories is the natural shape of a target.

Attribution. None. Microsoft calls the network indicators unattributed. It links to an earlier post about a group it tracks as Storm zero nine forty using A R T E X in a ConnectWise supply chain compromise, but does not say this is the same group.

The timeline. The agentic searches of GitHub were first seen in August. The earliest successful sign ins as service principals, the identities that applications use, came on the twenty eighth of October from an address Microsoft names. Publication was on the fifth of November.

How it gets in. Not with an exploit, but with a search. The agents combine a target organisation's name with words like secret, or the names of cloud services and A I providers, and look for configuration files and scripts that someone has pushed to a public repository with the credentials still inside. Then they log in, as the application.

What it does next. The agents chain ordinary tools: network scanners, web clients, Python and a headless browser. They retry intelligently when a call fails. They run commands on virtual machines, search Microsoft Graph, and harvest whatever the application can reach: storage keys, app settings, connection strings, Kubernetes credentials, keys to A I providers, and applications with access to Azure's A I platform. They list and exfiltrate Key Vault secrets and reach into storage and DevOps. In one case, the whole journey from a GitHub search to stolen Key Vault data took about five minutes. The security firm Todyl, separately, describes the same address signing into Entra and Azure DevOps through managed service providers, reaching at least twenty seven organisations, downloading eighty one files and touching about two thousand two hundred and seventy nine mailbox items.

The indicators. Four I P addresses, which are in the brief: a primary address used for sign ins and cloud management operations, a second that is a Tor exit node, a third that embedded shell commands in its user agent, and a fourth that reconnoitred Azure A I services. No domains or file hashes. This is not malware. It is a stranger with your key.

What to hunt for, from Microsoft. One: search service principal sign ins, Graph audit events and cloud app events since the twenty eighth of October for those addresses, and note that Microsoft's own first query leaves out the second address, so add it. Two: watch for the Entra error codes that mean someone is presenting an expired or invalid client secret; a failure followed by a success is urgent. Three: one address authenticating as three or more service principals, or one application requesting tokens for cloud management, Key Vault, storage and Graph within a short window. Four: in Azure DevOps, new personal access tokens and their first use, pipeline edits and unfamiliar user agents. And three hunts of my own, labelled as such: scan the firm's public GitHub organisations for secrets mentioning the firm's name; inventory the vendor multitenant apps consented in each tenant; and alert on Key Vault listing by service principals from outside the corporate network.

The response, from Microsoft. Block the exact addresses for workload identities. Revoke sessions, tokens and keys. Remove every secret, certificate and federated credential on an affected application, including any the attacker added, remembering that tokens already issued stay valid until they expire. Rotate Key Vault secrets. Move to managed identities, which have no secret to leak. Turn on push protection in GitHub. And, a nice touch, consider decoys and tarpits to waste the agents' time.

The broader point. For twenty years we have told developers not to commit secrets to public repositories, and for twenty years some of them have. What has changed is the reader. A human attacker might find one leaked key a week. An agent reads everything, tries everything, and is in the vault before the developer has finished their coffee.
