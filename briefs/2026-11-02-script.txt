## Morning Signal. Monday, the second of November.

Good morning. Monday. The clocks went back yesterday, so you were given an extra hour this weekend. Others used theirs differently. Someone slipped a backdoor into a coding assistant that developers install without a second thought. Someone else has been walking in and out of Ivanti's front door, one command at a time, without ever leaving a trace behind. And in Canada, two more of the country's grandest law firms have admitted that a friendly voice from the I T department was nothing of the sort.

This is Morning Signal for Monday, the second of November. Because it's Monday, the window is seventy two hours, from about five on Friday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief carries every source link, and the organisations table has ninety four rows, which tells you how busy a quiet weekend can be.

A housekeeping note. Now that daylight saving has ended, seven in the morning Eastern is twelve noon U T C. Nothing changes for you. The episode will still be waiting when you wake.

Three things.

## The top three

Number one. A poisoned copy of the Claude Code extension sat on the Cursor marketplace for about eleven hours last Thursday. It planted an S S H key on the machines that installed it, tried to open a tunnel back to the attacker, and set itself up to survive a reboot. Removing the extension does not remove any of that. This is today's deep dive, and if your firm lets engineers or consultants use Cursor, it is the first thing to check this morning.

Number two. Ivanti Connect Secure has two more zero days. They chain with the one disclosed in the middle of October to give an unauthenticated attacker root on the appliance. The federal deadline for one of them is today. And the attacker behind it leaves no implant, so a patched box tells you nothing about whether you were visited.

Number three. The Canadian law firm campaign has grown. Bennett Jones and Borden Ladner Gervais have joined McCarthy Tétrault and Davies as confirmed victims. Two American firms have appeared on the Silent Ransom Group's leak site. And Microsoft has published two intrusions that used exactly the same opening, a message on Teams from someone claiming to be the help desk, and ended with the attacker holding certificates for eleven privileged accounts.

Let's begin where most of the damage is done. With people.

## Breaches

The Canadian law firms first, because this is the story that looks most like us.

Four firms are now confirmed. Bennett Jones says two accounts in its business services group were compromised, reported on the nineteenth of October, and that client revenue reports and descriptions of mandates were accessed. It says its document management and accounting systems were not touched. Borden Ladner Gervais, B L G, says a portion of its Microsoft cloud environment was accessed, and it has Mandiant on the case. You'll recall McCarthy Tétrault, four accounts and about thirty one thousand files, and Davies, one account. Stikeman Elliott and Gowling W L G were targeted, but neither has confirmed a compromise.

The method has not changed, which is the point. A message on Microsoft Teams from someone posing as internal I T. A phone call that appears to come from the I T department's own number. A sign-in page that looks right and quietly relays the multi factor prompt. At least seven employees across the four firms said yes. No vulnerability was required. No ransom demand has been reported, and no one has named the actor.

Now put that next to what Microsoft published on Friday. Two intrusions at customers it did not name. Same opening, a fake help desk on Teams. In the first wave three users were approached. About ninety minutes later, sixteen more. From there the attacker abused Active Directory Certificate Services, the misconfigurations the industry calls E S C one and E S C eight, and obtained certificates for eleven privileged accounts. Then a D C Sync to pull password hashes from the domain controller, and R clone to carry the data out over S F T P. Nobody has linked the two campaigns. They don't need to be linked to teach the same lesson. The help desk is the new perimeter, and Teams is the door most firms leave on the latch.

## Exploited vulnerabilities

The K E V catalogue grew by two on Friday, both Ivanti, and has been silent since.

Here's the Ivanti picture. In the middle of October, Volexity disclosed a zero day in Connect Secure being used by an actor it calls U T A zero five seven six. Ivanti patched it. Now Ivanti has disclosed two more flaws, a privilege escalation and a file upload, and together the three give an unauthenticated attacker root. Here's the part that should make you set down your coffee. Devices fully patched against the October flaw were still exposed to this chain. The fixed versions are twenty five point one point one point three and twenty two point eight R two point six.

The deadline for federal agencies to fix the privilege escalation is today. C I S A has flagged it for forensic triage.

What makes this actor unusual is restraint. There's no web shell. No implant. Every time it wants to run a command, it runs the whole exploit chain again, and passes the command and the answer inside the exploit's own encrypted traffic. It cleans the logs and evades Ivanti's own integrity checker. Once inside, it takes the credentials stored on the appliance, including the L D A P account used to talk to Active Directory, dumps directory metadata, and runs tools that hunt for L A P S passwords and BitLocker recovery keys. It also does Kerberoasting and password spraying. Shadowserver sees more than five thousand of these appliances exposed to the internet.

So the advice is not just patch. Patch, then assume the credentials on the box are gone. Rotate them. Look in Active Directory for unusual Kerberos ticket requests and for reads of L A P S and BitLocker attributes. A burglar who leaves nothing behind is still a burglar.

GitLab. The deadline for its maximum severity flaw passed on Sunday. GitLab has now published detections for self managed customers after seeing exploitation attempts, and Shadowserver counts about two thousand eight hundred exposed community edition servers. I have to flag a conflict. On Thursday I gave you one fixed version for the nineteen point three branch, and on Friday a slightly lower one. Today BleepingComputer lists nineteen point four point two, nineteen point three point five and nineteen point two point eight. Rather than trust me or anyone else, check GitLab's own release notes before you sign off on a server.

Two flaws are being exploited that aren't on the K E V list. WordPress core, versions four point seven through seven point one point three, has a flaw in how it stages files during upgrades. It's fixed in seven point one point four, and Wordfence blocked nearly sixty three thousand attacks in a single day. And Drupal Commerce has an access bypass that Drupal itself says is being exploited in the wild against online stores.

Patch status, briefly. Palo Alto has now shipped all twenty of its PAN O S hotfixes for the P H P two Shell chain. The Linux kernel T C P flaw still has no fix on Ubuntu, Red Hat or AlmaLinux, though Debian and Oracle have shipped one. VMware's S D D C Manager still has only a temporary workaround for the Brotli flaw.

## A I attacks and trends

The most elegant thing I read this weekend came from Cisco Talos, and I use the word elegant with some distaste.

It's an infostealer called VOLTA. It does what stealers do. Browser passwords and cookies, Slack, Discord and Telegram sessions, Microsoft O Auth tokens, S S H keys, cloud configs, crypto wallets. It even gets past Chrome's newer cookie encryption by launching Chrome in headless mode. But then it does something new. It writes up a report on the victim and sends it to a large language model through OpenRouter, asking, in effect, how much is this person worth. The default model is a Claude model, with two others as fallbacks, and it checks the reply for refusals. The prompt casts the model as a forensic intelligence analyst drafting a target assessment. The answer is packed up and sent to Telegram with everything else. The criminals have hired an analyst. They simply don't pay it a salary. Talos thinks VOLTA is probably a builder sold to many customers. For hunting, look for Chrome launched headless by something that isn't a developer tool, and for OpenRouter traffic from programs that have no business talking to it.

CrowdStrike published its own view of ARTEX, the A I driven intrusion framework we covered last week. It attributes this campaign to a China nexus actor it calls VAPOR PANDA. Since the seventeenth of October, the operation has probed more than two hundred organisations in at least fourteen countries, with confirmed post exploitation at nine. The targets include foreign ministries, a Middle Eastern interior ministry, and nineteen American organisations in pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, defence, nuclear and finance. The details are what stay with you. The agents were told to look for hosts that didn't have CrowdStrike's own sensor installed. In one intrusion the planning agents consumed about one point four billion input tokens. And one way in was a set of keys from a twenty twenty two mail server export that nobody had ever rotated. The machines are tireless. They are still relying on our forgetfulness.

The A I developer platforms had a rough month. Northflank, a cloud deployment platform, told customers on Friday to assume every environment variable had been decrypted and every secret stolen, and later that night reported misuse of customers' A I keys. Wiz counts at least nine incidents at A I developer platforms in October. In two cases it investigated, stolen credentials were used to make about two and a half million calls to Amazon Bedrock, roughly a hundred thousand dollars of someone else's compute.

A smaller but sharper idea. A fake Solana starter kit on GitHub, close to three hundred stars and a top search result, contains instructions in its readme aimed not at people but at A I coding agents. Run this PowerShell. The script installs NetSupport, a remote access tool. Prompt injection is no longer theoretical. It has a GitHub account.

On the Accenture story from last week, about an A I agent allegedly connected to a Pentagon network, Accenture's position is that the allegations were thoroughly and independently investigated and found to be without merit. The Defense Department won't comment. Nothing further from Congress.

## Deep dive: the poisoned Claude Code extension

Let me tell you about a key with a very modest name.

On Thursday the twenty ninth of October, at about half past nine in the morning, U T C, a new version of the Claude Code extension appeared on the marketplace used by Cursor, the A I code editor. It carried Anthropic's extension identifier. It looked, to anyone who glanced, exactly like the real thing. Two versions, in fact, two point one point three one two and two point one point three one three. Developers installed it, or their editors updated to it automatically, as editors do. It stayed up until about half past eight that evening. Eleven hours. The Hacker News reports Cursor saying at most five thousand users were affected.

The extension did everything Claude Code is supposed to do. But before any of that, about sixty six extra lines of JavaScript ran first.

They began by taking a polite inventory. The machine's name, the user, the operating system, the home and working directories, the email address configured for git. And then the names, only the names, of up to forty environment variables that looked like they might hold something valuable. Anything with key, token, secret, pass, A W S or G C P in the name. Not the values. Just a list of what's in the safe, without opening the door. That list was sent, in plain text, over H T T P, to a server at port eight zero nine nine.

Then came the key. The code looked in the user's authorized keys file for an S S H key with the comment s v c dash telemetry. If it wasn't there, it added it. A modest little name. Service telemetry. The sort of thing an administrator would see and scroll past. Then it tried to switch on the S S H server, if the machine had one, and opened a reverse tunnel back to the attacker, so that the attacker could reach in through the developer's own outbound connection. On Mac and Linux it preferred a tool called auto S S H, which reconnects on its own. On Windows it used a hidden PowerShell loop that tried again every sixty seconds.

And then it made itself at home. On a Mac, a launch agent named com dot apple dot sysmond, which sounds like something Apple would ship. On Linux, a line in the user's crontab to run at every reboot. On Windows, a scheduled task called SysMon, which sounds like a well known Microsoft tool, with a fallback in the startup folder. Each with small helper files tucked into a hidden config folder.

Cursor says that on most Mac and Windows machines, the lack of admin rights stopped the tunnel from opening. But the key and the persistence were still written. And here's the part I'd underline. Uninstalling the extension doesn't remove any of it. The key stays in authorized keys. The task still runs at boot. The guest has left the party, but he's kept a key to the house.

Cursor took the marketplace down, rebuilt it, rotated its keys and took the attacker's server offline. Version two point one point three one four is clean. The Visual Studio Marketplace, the npm package and Open V S X were not affected, and Anthropic says its own systems weren't breached. The question no one has answered is how the attacker got the right to publish under that name on Cursor's marketplace in the first place. No actor has been named. Microsoft is careful to say that its mapping of techniques does not establish attribution.

So what do you do this morning.

First, find out whether anyone in your fleet ran those two versions. Look in Cursor's extension directories for the Claude Code folder with versions three one two or three one three.

Second, search every authorized keys file, on Macs, Linux and Windows, for s v c dash telemetry, or for the key fingerprint in the brief. Look for S S H processes with a remote forward to port twenty two whose parent is Cursor or Node. Look for S S H servers that have recently been switched on, on laptops that never needed one. And look for any connection to the attacker's address, which is in the brief, since the twenty ninth. Microsoft has published hunting queries that look for the launch agent, the scheduled task, the startup file and the helper files by name. Match on names, because file hashes may be missing from some events.

Third, if you find it, remove the key, the launch agent, the cron entry or task, the startup file and the helper files, and kill the tunnel. Cursor has published clean up scripts for Mac and Windows. Read them before you run them, which is advice I'd give about any script that arrives in the middle of an incident.

Fourth, and this is where the polite inventory comes back to bite. The attacker has a list of which secrets lived on each machine, even if not the secrets themselves. From a clean device, rotate the cloud keys, git tokens and A I A P I keys that those machines held. The list tells the attacker what to go looking for next. Make sure what they find has already expired.

And then the policy conversation. Developer tools are now one of the most privileged things in the building. A coding assistant can read the source, run commands and hold the keys to the cloud. This weekend's lesson is that extensions should come from an approved list of publishers, that new versions should wait a few days before they roll out, and that outbound S S H from a laptop is something to allow on purpose, not by default.

I'll leave you with one thought. The cleverest part of this wasn't the tunnel or the persistence. It was the name. Not malware. Not backdoor. Service telemetry. Most attacks succeed not because nobody looks, but because when somebody does look, what they see seems perfectly reasonable.
