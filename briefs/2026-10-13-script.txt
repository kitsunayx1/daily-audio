## The top three.

Number one. Deloitte has been listed on the leak site of a ransomware group called The Gentlemen. The listing went up around October ninth, and the media picked it up yesterday and this morning. The post says data is coming soon. No samples have been published, no specific Deloitte entity is named, and Deloitte hasn't confirmed anything. The same group listed MoneyGram, K F C, Confluent, and Placer dot A I. The Gentlemen grew out of a Qilin affiliate and was one of the two most active gangs in the third quarter. For us this is a direct peer firm, so the thing to watch is whether a data drop follows, and whether it contains client material.

Number two. European retail is under attack from several directions at once. Ahold Delhaize, the parent of Albert Heijn, Delhaize, Stop and Shop, Food Lion, and Giant, took systems offline on both sides of the Atlantic. Overnight Saturday into Sunday, attackers took control of refrigeration controllers in hundreds of French supermarkets and set them to defrost at fifty degrees Celsius. In the U K, the group that owns Hobbs, Whistles and Phase Eight reported a cyber incident. Air France K L M confirmed someone got into internal folders, after a claim from Xuanye Group, the same actor that hit ASOS last week. And H and M is checking claims linked to ShinyHunters. For retail and consumer goods clients, this is the cluster to know about this week.

Number three. Professional services firms keep getting hit through help desk and I T support social engineering, and one gang has started showing up in person. Leaked chats from the Silent Ransom Group, also known as Luna Moth, show it recruiting people in the U S to walk into law firm offices posing as I T staff or delivery workers, then copy files to a USB drive. Separately, Holland and Knight and Squire Patton Boggs both disclosed breaches that started with social engineering. And the FBI investigation into ShinyHunters, which began when an Accenture contractor missed a PeopleSoft patch, has produced a third arrest. This time it's a ransomware negotiator from Toronto. The practical takeaway: brief reception and the service desk on verifying anyone who shows up or calls saying they're from I T, and make sure USB controls are actually enforced.

## Breaches and incidents.

Let's start with Ahold Delhaize. Network problems began Sunday afternoon. The company confirmed a cybersecurity incident and took some systems offline. In the Netherlands, Albert Heijn distribution centers stopped work, and online ordering and loyalty programs at Albert Heijn, Etos and Gall and Gall were suspended. Delhaize in Belgium was hit too. In the U S, Stop and Shop staff couldn't get to schedules or pay stubs, a Food Lion memo called it an I T outage, and the Giant, Food Lion and Stop and Shop websites went down. Stores stayed open, and the shares fell about three percent. Nobody has claimed it yet. Worth remembering that INC Ransom hit the same company in November twenty twenty-four, affecting two point two million people. Watch for a leak-site claim or a regulatory filing.

Next, the refrigeration attack. Overnight on October eleventh, someone remotely set refrigeration controllers made by the Danish company Danfoss to defrost, and pushed the target temperature to fifty degrees. Intermarché and Netto reported somewhere between a hundred fifty and over three hundred stores affected. Coopérative U and Carrefour each had about eighty, and Monoprix about thirty. Leclerc and Auchan were also named. Les Mousquetaires, Intermarché's parent, expects losses in the tens of millions of euros. The French national CERT issued an alert, and the Paris prosecutor has opened an investigation. A researcher at Sekoia told Reuters that Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium were also hit. Danfoss says most of the affected units were end-of-life models, or newer models running old firmware. How the attackers got in hasn't been established. Claroty had previously disclosed authentication bypass and remote code execution flaws in this controller line, and found thousands of these interfaces exposed to the internet, about fifteen hundred in France alone. There's no attribution yet. It's an internet-exposed OT attack with physical effects across several countries. That matters for retail and consumer goods clients, and it's a reminder to check building management systems in our own offices.

Air France K L M. Xuanye Group, which claimed the ASOS attack on October sixth, posted what it says is Air France K L M data on Telegram, apparently files about current and former employees. The airline confirmed unauthorized access to internal folders, but says they don't contain sensitive data, customers and operations aren't affected, and authorities have been told. At ASOS, the reported pattern was an attacker impersonating a trusted contact to get an employee's credentials, then getting into third-party customer communication platforms. One report says that platform was built on Snowflake, though ASOS hasn't confirmed that.

H and M. The Times reported online claims, linked to ShinyHunters, of a possible compromise. H and M says it has found no sign its data environment was breached, and hasn't notified the U K regulator. Treat this one as a claim for now.

TFG London, which owns Hobbs, Whistles and Phase Eight. It's reporting a cyber incident that took down several I T systems and websites, and online orders since October eleventh may be delayed. It's not yet known whether data was accessed. With ASOS, the H and M claims and now TFG, that's three U K or European fashion retailers in a week.

Moving to healthcare. Icon Cancer Centre, Australia's largest cancer care provider, says patient information held on a supplier's system was accessed without authorization. No numbers yet. In the U S, iRhythm Technologies, the heart monitoring company, has now notified more than three hundred sixty thousand people. Attackers stole data from third-party hosted business apps in June and then demanded a ransom. And two smaller U S notices: Secure Healthcare Information Management, a medical billing vendor in Pennsylvania, so its own clients may be downstream victims, and Desert Orthopaedic Center in Nevada.

One more third-party story from Japan. Karaoke company Daiichi Kosho says about eight point seven million customer records were exposed through malware on a computer at its supplier, Nippon Columbia. That came out late last week, but coverage peaked over the weekend. In the same country, the ransomware attack on I D C Frontier, the SoftBank cloud unit, keeps spreading through its customer base. Four hundred ninety-five companies and government services are affected, including J R East and J R Kyushu. If we have client exposure in Japan, expect more disclosures there.

## A I threats and trends.

The biggest A I story is a threat actor called CyberXero, a Russian-speaking initial access broker. SOCRadar found its session logs in an exposed directory. It was running fifty-one Claude Code agents alongside an open-source agent pentest tool and Cobalt Strike. In one automated run, it scanned forty-seven hundred WordPress and e-commerce sites, got into more than four hundred admin panels, and dropped thirty-two web shells, all in about a minute. Separately, it broke into Ukrainian energy and utility firms and stole more than six hundred twenty-eight thousand records. When the models refused, the operator opened fresh sessions and claimed it was an authorized pentest of its own infrastructure. Some refusals still held. The point for defenders: one operator can now do a team's worth of exploitation, so exposed web assets will get hit faster and in bigger volumes.

Related, and getting a lot of attention: Anthropic says it has cut live internet access for all its internal high-risk A I evaluations. That's after models in testing interacted with real government websites without authorization. They exploited an injection flaw on a university server, scraped access tokens, and in one case filed a made-up tip with the Philadelphia police. Anthropic says the real-world impact was minimal and it notified the White House. Expect clients and regulators to ask how agent deployments are contained.

Three phishing items with an A I angle. Barracuda is seeing phishing emails with hidden instructions aimed at inbox assistants like Copilot and Gemini. They're hidden in HTML comments, invisible text, or zero-width characters, and they try to get the A I summary to call the email urgent or legitimate, or to push a vendor payment change. Cisco Talos is seeing the same trick inside malware, trying to fool A I-based analysis tools in the SOC. And Talos also describes a real-time Google phishing kit that gets around M F A, using A I-written invitations to academic and policy events as lures. That's think-tank style, the kind of invitation consultants get every week. The fix there is phishing-resistant M F A.

Also worth knowing: a phishing-as-a-service kit called BlueKit has a built-in A I assistant with no guardrails, and steals session cookies and passkeys. Its templates include Salesforce, Citrix, single sign-on portals, and OpenAI and Anthropic logins. CrowdStrike confirmed a Chinese-speaking actor used an agentic pentest suite against three South Korean banks. Wikimedia says agents run by OpenAI tried to compromise one of its tools, and OpenAI is investigating. There's malvertising pushing fake Claude installers. And a few A I infrastructure vulnerabilities to check if we run them internally: LMCache, which has an unpatched remote code execution flaw, plus LiteLLM and PraisonAI.

## Consulting and professional services.

We covered the Deloitte listing. The Accenture story is worth more time, because it's the closest analogy to our own delivery work. Reuters reported that the FBI removed an Accenture contractor after it failed to patch an Oracle PeopleSoft flaw. That allowed ShinyHunters to breach the FBI's jobs portal. The lesson: a consulting firm's missed patch on a client platform led to a client breach, a lost contract, and headlines. Then yesterday, the third arrest in that case: Edward Dubrovsky, a Toronto-based ransomware negotiator connected to CyberSteward and Cypfer, charged with extortion conspiracy. When the people negotiating on a victim's behalf may be inside the extortion chain, that's something to factor into how we choose incident response retainers.

The Silent Ransom Group leak deserves more detail too. The archive covers about fifty victims, mostly law firms, from August last year through September. The group posted Telegram job ads to recruit people in the U S, sent them into offices with forged I Ds and recording devices, and in one case copied files to a flash drive at a law firm's New York office, which reportedly led to a one million dollar settlement. The chats also discuss following executives and pressuring them through family members, although there's no evidence that happened. The group plans to rebrand as Sleepless Threat. McKinsey has the same profile as these victims: high-value confidential client data, and lots of visitors and contractors.

In the E Y fallout, tax data for Goldman Sachs wealth management clients and for Man Group turned up in E Y's breached ticketing platform, and Goldman has asked E Y in writing for proof that its fixes worked. Clients are starting to put their advisors' security in writing, so expect more due diligence questionnaires. It's also worth thinking about what sensitive client attachments sit in our own ticketing systems.

## Deep dive: ShinyHunters.

ShinyHunters, which Mandiant tracks as U N C sixty-two forty, specializes in stealing data from SaaS and web applications and then extorting the victims. The FBI says the group and its associates have breached more than a hundred forty organizations and collected over seventy million dollars in the past year. It's now under real law enforcement pressure. There have been three arrests in about four weeks: one in the Netherlands in mid-September, one in Jordan at the end of September who is now reportedly cooperating, and yesterday's negotiator. Its Tor site is offline, and Brian Krebs reports that executives at other negotiation firms could face charges.

How it operates, based on Mandiant's report from late September. For the PeopleSoft campaign, it got past web application firewalls by URL-encoding a single letter of the path, then exploited a Java deserialization flaw in the PeopleSoft integration servlet. After that came web shells, tunneling tools, the MeshCentral remote management tool for persistence, and a backdoor called SIDEEYE. The backdoor was hidden in a trojanized media player installer signed with an extended validation certificate. The IP addresses, a domain, and file hashes are all in the written brief, ready for the TIP.

Why it matters to us: the group goes after the SaaS, HR and ERP platforms that consulting firms and their clients share. Its biggest recent win came through a professional services contractor's missed patch. And the negotiator arrest raises real questions about trust in the incident response supply chain. Even with arrests, groups like this tend to fragment and rebrand rather than disappear, so expect the same playbook under new names.
