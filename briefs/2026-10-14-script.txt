There's an old habit among people who keep secrets. They check the room before they speak. Look under the table, behind the painting, inside the lamp. Most people find it charming, a little paranoid, a relic of another century. And then, every so often, someone finds a recording device tucked inside a roll of toilet paper in a conference room in San Francisco. And suddenly the old habits don't look quite so quaint.

## The top three.

First. Ivanti Connect Secure. If you own one, and a great many organizations do, put down the coffee. Volexity has documented a new actor, which it calls U T A zero five seven six, chaining three bugs to get root on fully patched V P N appliances. Not old ones. Fully patched ones. Exploitation goes back to around the first of October. And this is the part I admire, in the way one admires a well-made lock pick. The attacker leaves no web shell and no backdoor. It changes the appliance only in memory, then tidies up after itself, so Ivanti's own Integrity Checker sees nothing at all. Meanwhile it takes the L D A P credentials stored on the box, goes looking for LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys, and sprays passwords across the domain. CISA added it to the Known Exploited list yesterday and gave federal agencies until Friday to patch and do forensic triage. And one link in that chain still has no fix. Ivanti says it's coming in the next few weeks. Patch what you can, and hunt as though you've already been visited.

Second. Patch Tuesday arrived, and it arrived hungry. Roughly fourteen hundred vulnerabilities, depending on whose arithmetic you prefer. That's a record, beating September's nine hundred and seventy-four, which was itself a record. Only one is confirmed exploited. It's a Windows Shell flaw that strips the Mark of the Web from a downloaded file. That little tag is what makes Windows suspicious of an attachment, so without it, SmartScreen and Protected View stay quiet. A crafted file arrives by email or chat, someone opens it, and the guard dog doesn't bark. It's in CISA's catalog, also with a Friday deadline. There's also an Outlook flaw rated nine point eight that fires from the Reading Pane, without the user doing anything. With fourteen hundred items on the menu, the only sane approach is to sort by what's actually being exploited, not by how long the list is.

Third. Some threats walk in through the door, or get hired. Yesterday an OpenAI engineer was charged after that recording device turned up in a conference room. The Justice Department announced it had dismantled North Korean I T worker laptop farms across Texas, more than seventy American employers deceived. And North Korea's Kimsuky group was caught posing as conference organizers and policy researchers to hook experts. Three different doors, one lesson. Sweep the rooms where sensitive client work happens. Check who you're actually onboarding, and where their laptop is shipped. And when a flattering invitation arrives offering to pay for your travel, verify it some other way before you reply.

## Breaches and incidents.

Meta. Someone stole a single employee's credentials and used them to wander into some of Meta's private GitHub repositories. What came out was mostly source code, about ninety-nine percent of it. The remaining one percent was personal data on roughly fifteen hundred people, mostly U S employees, and it was sitting in those repositories against Meta's own policy. Meta has rotated its secrets and is adding scanning for personal data. No one has been named. There's a lesson in that one percent. The breach was a stolen password. What made it a privacy incident was someone, at some point, putting data where it didn't belong. For any firm with build teams, and we have several, it's worth asking what's quietly sitting in our own repositories.

South Africa. Ampath Laboratories, one of the country's largest pathology providers, confirmed an attack that likely compromised patient data. A group calling itself ByteToBreach claims eighty million patient records. That number is theirs, not Ampath's, and extortionists are not known for modesty. Still, if it's even partly true, it's one of the largest healthcare exposures the continent has seen. It's a POPIA matter and relevant to healthcare work out of Johannesburg.

Now one that sits uncomfortably close to home. Liberating Research is a U K market research firm that pays patients and doctors to answer medical surveys. It left an Elasticsearch database open to the internet. UpGuard found personal details on about a hundred and ten thousand people, and health data, diagnoses and medications, on about seventy-four thousand of them. The records go back to twenty fifteen. The company's website, I'm told, describes its data as completely anonymized. It hasn't responded. If you work with survey panels, expert networks or research vendors, and in this business we all do, this is the third-party risk in its plainest form.

A few shorter ones. The U K Housing Ombudsman lost its case management system after an incident at an unnamed supplier, and has notified the regulator. Park Dental Partners filed an updated 8-K with the SEC, upgrading its incident from accessed to stolen, and now faces four class actions. Photon Health, a prescription network, was breached through a zero-day in Metabase, the business intelligence tool, and a group called Direwolf has claimed it. Healthcare Highways in Texas lost Social Security numbers and claims data, with the Chaos group claiming two hundred thirty-five gigabytes. And Unit 42 described an unnamed European bank breached through a V P N gateway that a third party managed for it. The attacker logged in with stolen credentials, sprayed passwords at admin accounts, and tried a WSUS exploit before being contained.

Denmark. The national population register, eight point eight million people, was breached last month. We now hear how. The attacker reportedly came in through accounts belonging to a small I T vendor with legitimate lookup access, and at least three of those accounts, including an admin account, used the password one, two, three, four, five, six. One does sometimes despair.

And the Danes have a second problem. The Fehmarnbelt tunnel, the eighteen-kilometer link being built under the Baltic between Denmark and Germany, had its construction I T knocked out. Staff lost email and Wi-Fi, site access control failed and workers were signed in by hand, and vessels off the Danish coast stopped working. Nobody has claimed it. The chair of Denmark's digital security council declined to rule out hybrid warfare, and Danish authorities raised their threat level for destructive attacks to high last month. Large infrastructure programs make attractive targets for people who want to send a message without firing a shot.

Updates on yesterday's stories. Ahold Delhaize is in what it calls a controlled relaunch, says it has no evidence so far of data theft, and its stores are open. One Dutch headline reads as if theft was found, so treat that point as unsettled. On the French supermarket fridges, more than three hundred fifty stores were hit. ## A I.

Anthropic published research suggesting small, open A I models may soon be able to copy themselves onto cheap internet-connected devices. In its tests, agents spread models across exposed Android TV boxes, and in one setup reached every node in an emulated network four times out of five. It hasn't demonstrated a fully autonomous worm on real hardware, and setup took days. It expects models of thirty billion parameters or less to get there by the end of this year. The Register, never shy, called it fearmongering. Either way, it's a fresh argument for keeping the cheap gadgets off the office network.

Volexity also believes, with medium confidence, that the Ivanti attacker used large language models to build its exploit and run its post-exploitation scripts. Put that next to the Patch Tuesday numbers and you see the trend. A I is helping defenders find bugs faster, and it's helping attackers use them faster too. I B M, via SecurityWeek, now puts A I-generated attacks at just over a fifth of everything they see. And more than four hundred scientists, led by Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, signed a letter calling for frontier models to be air-gapped while they're being built.

## Deep dive: U T A zero five seven six.

Let's spend a moment on our new acquaintance, because it's a portrait of where this craft is heading. Volexity can't tie U T A zero five seven six to any known group. It leans, with low confidence, toward criminal rather than state motives. It thinks, with medium confidence, that the actor uses A I to write its tools. And it's highly confident the actor was getting ready to move deeper into the network when it was interrupted.

The method is elegant. It sends a request with a forged header that lets it skip authentication. Then it wins a race against the appliance's own cleanup routine to run a Perl script disguised as a zip file. That loads Python in memory and patches a system library, again only in memory, to become root. There's no persistence, because it doesn't need any. It simply comes back the same way. Then it gets to work. It exports Active Directory, hunts for LAPS passwords and BitLocker keys, Kerberoasts service accounts, sprays passwords, and runs tcpdump, renamed to look like a harmless system process, to sniff credentials off the wire. When it's done, it edits the logs and restores everything it touched. A thoughtful guest. It even makes the bed.

The indicators are in the brief, including an IP address, the request pattern, file paths and the fake process name. The advice is straightforward. Patch. Don't trust the Integrity Checker. Do memory and network forensics. Treat any connection the appliance itself starts as suspicious. And if you were exposed, rotate the L D A P service accounts, the LAPS passwords and the BitLocker keys. It's tedious work, and that's precisely why attackers count on nobody doing it.
