## The top three

Number one. Binance has lost about six point three billion dollars, the largest theft of cryptocurrency on record, and the blockchain analysts say it was North Korea.

At five past eight last night, U T C, which is just after four in the afternoon in Washington, Binance's hot wallets began emptying. Ten different blockchains. Most of it gone in about twenty minutes, with a second wave around three hours later. TRM Labs puts the total at six point three six billion. Chainalysis says about five point nine. About two point seven to three billion has been frozen. And how? Binance's first account said a DevOps engineer's laptop may have been compromised, giving access to a service container used to submit withdrawals. Binance says customer funds are safe and it will cover the losses.

It's this morning's deep dive, so I'll say just this now. Six billion dollars, and the front door was probably a laptop.

The action. If our finance or treasury teams, or any client teams, have exposure to Binance, it's worth a call this morning. Expect Binance themed phishing for weeks. ## Deep dive: six billion dollars and a laptop

Now the deep dive. TraderTraitor and Binance.

Let me give you the timeline as TRM Labs reconstructed it, because the preparation tells you more than the theft.

Wednesday morning, three small withdrawals from Tornado Cash, the mixing service, fund an operator wallet. Not much. Just enough for fuel. Early Thursday, the attack code is tested on two wallets, and then rehearsed on a third. About four hours later, the same code is installed on a Binance hot wallet. They practised. Then they waited.

At five past eight, U T C, the X R P goes, from five wallets. At ten past, Solana and Optimism. At twenty past, eighteen thousand Bitcoin. And in the same few minutes, one transaction submits fifty pre signed authorisations on Binance's main Ethereum hot wallet.

That last detail is the clever one. Ethereum recently gained a feature that lets an ordinary wallet delegate its behaviour to a piece of code. The attacker had fifty such delegations signed in advance, each tied to a consecutive sequence number, so that whichever number the wallet was on when they struck, one of them would be valid. Once it took, the wallet itself became a sweeper. It didn't just send its balance out. It kept forwarding anything that arrived afterwards. At about twenty three minutes past eight, the whole Ethereum hot wallet went: a hundred and sixty seven thousand Ether and sixty three different tokens, including one point six billion in Tether.

Then the institutions moved. At one minute past nine, Tether froze nine hundred and seventy one million. At four past, Circle froze eight hundred and twenty three million. Ripple and World Liberty followed. At five past eleven a second wave hit two more chains, and just after midnight, Arbitrum's security council forcibly moved the attacker's holdings on its network out of reach.

What remains with the attacker, about two point four billion in Ether and Bitcoin, is now being laundered. Three hundred and twenty five million in stablecoins sold for Ether at more than a ten percent discount, which tells you how much of a hurry they're in. Sixty million deposited on a lending platform to borrow Ether against it, until the platform froze the market. Bitcoin moving down a peel chain, a little at a time. TRM expects bridges, swap services and over the counter brokers next.

Now, the question that matters to the rest of us. How did they get the signatures?

Binance's first account was a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, giving access to a service container used to submit withdrawals. Its later account was more cautious: additional points of breach, multiple factors, and no evidence a private key leaked. TRM's view is that the attacker most likely gained the ability to make Binance's signing system sign whatever it asked. Signatures across different chains, different key types and different signing schemes, all within about thirty minutes. Phishing a single employee doesn't explain that. Controlling the machine that signs does.

So the shape of the thing, as best we can see it, is this. An engineer's laptop. A container. A signing service that trusted what the container told it. And six billion dollars.

Now, what to take from it, if you don't run a crypto exchange.

First, every large organisation has a signing system of some kind. A payment approval service. A treasury platform. A code signing pipeline. The question is not whether it's secure in isolation. It's whether something that a developer can reach can ask it to sign.

Second, the hunting. Look at developer and DevOps laptops that can reach production containers, for new remote access tools, odd packages, and interactive sessions into payment or signing services outside change windows. Alert on bursts of signatures, or signing requests that skip the usual approval. And separate your signing paths by system and by value, so that one compromised service can't approve everything.

Third, the response, if you're close to this. Rotate credentials for anyone who can reach those paths. Rebuild suspect laptops rather than clean them. And review audit logs from the start of this week.

TRM has published no wallet addresses or indicators yet, and neither has Binance. I'll pass them on when they appear.

North Korea's thieves keep doing it the same way. Patience, rehearsal, and a person with access who opened the wrong thing. The money is new. The method is not.
