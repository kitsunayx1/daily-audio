## Morning Signal. Tuesday, the twentieth of October.

Good morning. Tuesday. The day the week stops pretending it's still Monday, and the problems that arrived over the weekend start sending their invoices.

This is Morning Signal for Tuesday, the twentieth of October. The window is the last twenty four hours, from about five o'clock yesterday morning, Eastern, until now. The written brief carries every source link, and the organisations table has fifty six rows this time, for your vendor and client lookups.

Number three. A fake Chat G P T assistant that teaches you to hack yourself.

Huntress and Forescout have published research on a platform being called ROMULUS. It starts with custom versions of Chat G P T, built inside OpenAI's own G P T store, with names like "Plus five point six". It also buys sponsored Google results. Both routes lead to a fake Cloudflare check that asks you to prove you're human by pasting a command into Windows. That command installs a remote access trojan. Huntress has handled more than forty incidents. One of the tricks involves a Siemens software tool, which is now being exploited in the wild. More on that in the deep dive.

The action. Tell people, again, in plain words. No genuine website ever asks you to paste a command to prove you're human. Not one. Not ever.

## Vulnerabilities

To the vulnerabilities.

First, a gap. C I S A's website wouldn't load for us again this morning, so I can't confirm the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue directly. The secondary trackers show nothing new since Friday. I'll believe that when I see it with my own eyes.

Now, Plex. Plex Media Server is the software a great many people run at home to stream their films and music around the house. A chain of four flaws, which the researchers named Plex2Shell, lets an attacker run code on a Plex server without logging in. The proof of concept was published on the sixteenth. By the seventeenth, people were using it. The payloads so far are a crypto miner, and a residential proxy, which rents out your home internet connection to strangers so they can look like you.

Why do we care? Because Plex runs on home networks. Including the home networks of people who work from home, on laptops that connect to ours. A compromised machine on the same network, quietly offering your home address to anyone who pays, is not a comfortable neighbour. The fix is version one point four three point three. If you run Plex, update it. If your colleagues do, tell them. The indicators are in the brief.

Siemens Software Center. A flaw that lets a malicious D L L sit next to a genuine Siemens program and be loaded by it. It's being exploited right now, as part of ROMULUS. The fixed version is four point zero point nine point eight. Most firms won't have it. Engineering and industrial clients might.

Oracle's quarterly patch release is due today. One thousand two hundred and eighty three fixes. Several rated a perfect ten for severity, in E-Business Suite, Fusion Middleware and J D Edwards, and nine point eights in PeopleSoft and MySQL. Oracle E-Business Suite exposed to the internet has been a mass exploitation target before. I'd patch that first and argue about the rest afterwards.

A curiosity, but a sobering one. A researcher, Quentin Kaiser, has found a flaw in Broadcom's Wi-Fi code, about sixteen years old, in the Wi-Fi Protected Setup feature. It gives root on a router without a password. There's no C V E. And on routers running an old operating system called eCos, it can't be fixed at all. Sixteen years. Some secrets keep remarkably well.

Critical, but not yet exploited. Google's open source Agent Mesh. A WordPress automation plugin called OttoKit. FreeBSD. GeoVision cameras. And Vanus. The identifiers are in the brief.

And the things that haven't moved. No named victims yet from the BeyondTrust zero day. No fix from Ivanti for its third bug. No exploitation of the GitLab flaw. And no attribution yet for the Palo Alto or Cisco activity. Silence, in this business, is rarely the same thing as peace.

## Software supply chain

Supply chain. A rich seam today.

Rank Math. One of the most popular S E O plugins for WordPress. Its paid version, Rank Math PRO, release three point zero point one two three, was trojanised. Anyone who downloaded it between about four in the afternoon on the fifteenth and about three in the morning on the sixteenth, Eastern time, got a backdoor with it.

Here's the unpleasant part. The fixed version, three point zero point one two four, does not remove what the backdoor left behind. It plants files, sets up hidden admin accounts, and adds a scheduled task. Updating the plugin is rather like changing the lock while the burglar is still upstairs. The advice is to delete the plugin's folder entirely and restore from a clean backup. Check marketing sites, campaign sites, and anything an agency built for you. GhostAction is back, aimed at developers. A phishing site posing as GitHub abuses the device login flow, the one where you type a short code to approve a new device, to steal tokens. And a credential stealer has been disguised as a font file and tucked into ninety four code repositories, belonging to sixty six owners. Elastic has found a fake Web three accelerator, calling itself Apex, pushing malicious packages to the npm registry. One of them is named to sound like a Copilot M C P tool. The package names and domains are in the brief.

## A I attacks and trends

Now, the machines.

Sekoia ran a honeypot, a decoy, dressed as an exposed Flowise server. Flowise is a tool for building A I agents and chatbots. In August, someone found it. They stole the A P I keys stored inside. Then they built their own malicious chatflow, one that called home to their server. Then they tried to jailbreak the model, to pull data out of the document store the agent used for answers, and to use the agent itself to scan the network for open ports. Sekoia thinks it was a Chinese-speaking group from the online gambling world.

What's interesting isn't the tools. It's the manners. This wasn't a scanner bumping into an open door. Someone came inside, sat down, and started asking the furniture questions. Exposed A I agent builders are now being worked by hand.

Reuters has a long piece on one hacker who used A I agents, and a few thousand dollars, to break into more than a hundred websites. BleepingComputer links it to a card skimming campaign: about six hundred thousand cards, across a hundred and nineteen sites. The Verge reports that John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins were among those hit. I haven't been able to verify that part, so treat it as reported, not established.

The Department of Homeland Security has banned DeepSeek across federal civilian agencies. If that wasn't already your firm's policy, it may be a useful reference point.

MITRE has released version twenty of ATT and CK. It now catalogues A I abuse techniques, M F A bypass methods, and attacks on water utilities, and it has added The Gentlemen ransomware group. If your detection team maps to ATT and CK, there's homework.

A sadder story. A former OpenAI engineer, Alfozan, has been detained in a spying case. The allegation is that he used one of OpenAI's own agents to work out which rooms the company's leadership used. And the court filing alleges WhatsApp contact with a Saudi royal. An insider using an A I agent for physical reconnaissance. That's a new sentence, and not a welcome one.

## Deep dive: ROMULUS, and the fake Chat G P T that teaches you to hack yourself

Now. Let's spend some time with ROMULUS.

The technique is called ClickFix, and its genius is that it doesn't need to break anything. It persuades you to do the breaking. A page shows you something familiar, a Cloudflare check, a CAPTCHA, the little box that asks if you're human. Then it says, to finish verifying, press the Windows key and R, paste this, and press enter. The command has already been copied to your clipboard for you. How thoughtful. You paste it, and you have just run PowerShell on your own machine, on behalf of a stranger, with your own permissions. No exploit. No vulnerability. Just good manners, turned against you.

What makes ROMULUS different is where it finds its victims. Huntress found malicious custom G P Ts, built inside OpenAI's own store, with names designed to sound like premium versions of Chat G P T. "Plus five point six", for example. Someone looking for a better assistant finds one, and the assistant, very helpfully, sends them to a verification page. That page is hosted on Google Sites. So the whole chain looks like OpenAI, Google and Cloudflare. Three of the most trusted names on the internet, holding the door open. The operators also buy sponsored results in Google search, so it works for people who never open Chat G P T at all.

Now, the installation. ROMULUS has two main routes.

The first uses installer files, M S I files, given long random names. Inside each is a genuine, digitally signed program from a respectable company, and next to it, a malicious D L L. When the genuine program starts, it loads the D L L sitting beside it, as programs do. That's called sideloading, and it's how you get a signed, trusted process to run your code. ROMULUS uses a Canon utility, a Stardock tool, and the Siemens Software Center we mentioned earlier, the one now officially recognised as being exploited. Three respectable programs, each carrying a passenger.

The second route uses a loader called LegionLoader. It arrives as a seven zip archive, and inside is an executable padded out to eight hundred and forty two megabytes. Why so big? Because many security tools won't scan a file that large. They skip it to save time. The attackers know that, and they've simply made the file too heavy to lift. There's something almost admirable in that. Almost.

What's at the end of the chain? A remote access trojan, which nobody has yet given a name. It can capture the screen, use the webcam, use the microphone, pull data out of seventeen different browsers, and search the disk for files. To find its command servers, it uses D N S over H T T P S, which hides the lookups inside ordinary encrypted web traffic. So the network team sees nothing unusual. Just another laptop, talking to the internet, like all the others.

And then persistence, which is where it gets stubborn. ROMULUS creates a Run key in the registry, so it starts when you log in. Every hundred and fifty seconds, it checks that the key is still there, and puts it back if not. It also creates a scheduled task, disguised as "Canon Configuration Reader" or "Stardock DeElevation Tool", and re-creates that every eight hundred and seventy five seconds. So if you delete the key and the task and leave the process running, you'll find both of them waiting for you a few minutes later, like a houseguest who keeps letting himself back in. Kill the process first. Then remove the rest.

How do you find it? Huntress and Forescout give a good list, and it's all in the brief. Let me give you the shape of it.

Look at the Run dialog history in the registry, the RunMRU key, for PowerShell commands with execution policy bypass, or a hidden window. That's the fingerprint of someone being talked into pasting. Look at PowerShell script block logging for a particular environment variable the scripts use. The exact string is in the brief. Look for web addresses written as a single decimal number instead of a normal I P address. That's an old trick to slip past filters. Look for the Windows installer running an M S I with a long random name out of the temporary folder. Look for signed programs running from the local app data programs folder, where they have no business being. Look for a command line seven zip being launched by PowerShell with a password. And look for executables bigger than five hundred megabytes sitting in folders users can write to. There aren't many legitimate ones.

The indicators. Eight I P addresses, four domains, and six file hashes, including the installers, the malicious D L Ls and the LegionLoader sample. All in the brief, ready for the blocklists.

If you find it, Huntress's advice is not to clean the machine but to rebuild it. And rotate the user's credentials, including anything saved in the browser, because the trojan will already have read them.

Now, why should this matter to us in particular?

Because the lure is exactly the kind of thing our people go looking for. Consultants use A I assistants all day. They're always looking for a better one, a faster one, a premium one. A G P T that promises to be the next version, hosted in the official store, is a very attractive invitation. And the defences we usually rely on, the browser warnings, the email filters, the attachment scanning, simply don't come into it. There's no attachment. There's no link in an email. There's just a person, alone with their keyboard, being asked politely to prove they're human.

So, three things.

First, the message to staff. Not a long training module. One sentence, repeated until people are bored of hearing it. No genuine website will ever ask you to paste anything into Windows to prove who you are.

Second, the technical control. Many firms can now restrict the Windows Run dialog, or alert on PowerShell launched from it. If yours can, this is the moment.

Third, the A I policy. If your firm has an approved list of A I tools, make sure people understand that a custom G P T in the public store is not an approved tool just because it lives inside one.

ROMULUS isn't the most sophisticated thing I've described on this programme. It doesn't need to be. It understands people. And it has realised that the most trusted place on the modern internet is a chat window with a friendly assistant in it.
