And because it's Friday, at the end you'll get the week in review, Friday to Thursday, for the team's weekly feed. The written brief has every source link and an organisations table. ## The top three

Number one. The contractor is the breach.

On Thursday, the Administrative Office of the U S Courts announced that a security incident at BAE Systems had stopped the Bankruptcy Noticing Center. That's the system that produces and sends notices for every federal bankruptcy court in the country. Every single one. One contractor. One system. And creditors across America are now waiting for letters that aren't coming.

The same day, we learned that an attack on an I T contractor called Senture had knocked out the phone lines for the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, the people who look after America's truckers. And a breach notice appeared in Massachusetts for National Insurance Company. It wasn't issued by the insurer. It was issued by its law firm, Squire Patton Boggs, whose own breach we discussed on Tuesday.

You see the pattern. The organisation in the headline is rarely the organisation that was actually broken into. For our restructuring and insolvency colleagues, expect delayed creditor notices. And expect someone, somewhere, to send phishing emails dressed as bankruptcy notices. They always follow the confusion.

Number two. The appliances keep falling, and the deadlines are here. C I S A's deadline for Ivanti Connect Secure is today. A public script that tests for the Ivanti bug appeared on Thursday. It only detects, by its own account, but it will make scanning easier, and your logs noisier. The deadlines for Palo Alto and Cisco's email gateway are tomorrow. And a new name has joined the list. AhsayCBS, a backup management console used widely by managed service providers, is being exploited and is now in the catalogue. If your backups are run by someone else, it's worth asking them a question this morning.

Number three. Spyware that arrives through an advertisement. No link. No click. Just an ad, served to the right phone. Citizen Lab and Confiant say an Israeli firm called Discover-Tech used targeted online advertising to deliver Candiru spyware. At least forty five devices belonging to Al Jazeera staff were targeted. Ads carrying the script appeared on networks belonging to NATO, the U N, E U bodies and the U S Defense Department, though an ad appearing on a network does not mean anyone there was infected. The practical step is refreshingly simple. Ad blocking or filtering D N S on executive and travel devices. Sometimes the best defence is to stop listening to strangers.

## Breaches and incidents

Let's take the BAE story a little further.

The systems down are the noticing center websites and the National Creditor Registration Service. PACER, the court records system, is not reported as affected. BAE says the incident is "isolated to the B N C system". There's no restoration timeline, no named attacker, and no statement on whether any data was taken. C I S A referred questions to the courts. The F B I declined to comment. When everyone politely points at someone else, the story usually has a second chapter.

The Senture incident has been running quietly since the sixth of October. The Department of Transportation says no safety administration data was compromised. Online tickets and chat kept working. One expert raised an eyebrow at an unverified claim that the attack was "A I powered". So shall I.

CenterPoint Energy, the Houston utility, told the Texas attorney general that one million, sixty two thousand Texans were affected by its breach. Names, addresses, Social Security numbers and driver's licence numbers. CenterPoint found out the way no company wants to. A hacker posted on a cybercrime forum, claiming about seven and a half million records. That larger figure is not verified. Customers in other states may be affected too.

Now, the U K Legal Aid Agency. The original breach was in the spring of last year. A forensic review published on Thursday found the stolen data goes back much further than anyone said. Back to nineteen seventy eight. About two hundred and fifty thousand records from before two thousand and seven, plus probation data. National Insurance numbers, financial assessments, bank details. And, in some Crown Court trials, the names of judges and of defence and prosecution lawyers. The hackers claimed data on more than two million people. That's unverified, and the minister says there's no sign it has been published. DataBreaches dot net says ShinyHunters claimed it. The government hasn't named anyone.

Why does that matter to us? Because lawyers' names, matched to the cases they worked on, are precisely the raw material for targeting a firm's partners. Data rarely stays in the drawer it was stolen from.

An update from Australia. Poole Group, the accounting and advisory firm LockBit listed on Wednesday, has confirmed an incident affecting part of its I T environment. It says it's contained, and it has notified the privacy regulator, the national cyber centre and the Australian Taxation Office. LockBit's countdown ends on the twenty fifth of October. A peer firm, holding client tax records, on a clock.

Also in Australia, Hunter Neurology, listed by N zero n, says the incident was at a third party technology provider that hosts its systems. N zero n claims patient scans, letters, and four weekly backups of the medical records system. Again, the supplier.

The French supermarket refrigeration attacks have grown. France's economy minister told lawmakers that about seven hundred of the country's roughly ten thousand supermarkets were affected. Earlier reports said three hundred and fifty. ANSSI, the French cyber agency, thinks the campaign is probably still running, and warns that resetting an old, exposed controller can simply invite the attacker back. The number of exposed Danfoss panels in France fell by about seventy percent in two days. Not because they were patched. Because they were unplugged. Sometimes the most effective security control is the plug.

And in Japan, Qilin has claimed Keio Corporation, the rail and hotel group, which confirmed a ransomware attack back in September. Its trains were fine. Its hotels less so.

Nothing new on Deloitte. The listing on The Gentlemen's site still says "data info, soon", with no countdown and no statement. The group hasn't posted a new victim since Monday. One thing earlier briefs missed. On Monday The Gentlemen also listed BigID, a data security vendor. A company whose business is knowing where everyone's sensitive data lives. I'll leave you to savour that.

## Vulnerabilities

AhsayCBS first, because it's new. It's the management server for Ahsay's backup software. Two flaws chained together, an authentication bypass and a remote code execution, give an attacker full system control. Huntress first saw exploitation on the seventh of October, and five organisations were hit by the next day. So far attackers have used it to install a web shell and a cryptocurrency miner. Which is the criminal equivalent of breaking into a bank and stealing the pens. But a door used to steal pens can be used to steal anything. The important detail is that the earlier fixes were incomplete. You need the newer hotfix, a reboot, and if you find indicators, a full re image, because Huntress saw hidden secondary backdoors. And the question for your suppliers, again. Do you run it?

Ivanti. Deadline today. The detection script works by sending the authentication bypass request. It doesn't write files or run code. The third bug in the chain, the privilege escalation, still has no reported fix.

Palo Alto, Cisco, Nextcloud. No attribution. No public exploit. No new indicators. Quiet. For now.

Langflow is a popular tool for building A I workflows. It has a nine point nine flaw. Any logged in user can add a so called M C P server, and the command attached to it simply runs. Every time the server list loads, it runs again. Previdian's honeypots logged more than seventeen hundred attempts since September, three hundred and sixteen on Thursday alone. The attackers guess common passwords, log in, and dump the secrets. Upgrade, turn off automatic login, and please, keep it off the internet.

A few patches with no exploitation yet. Microsoft pushed an out of band Office update on Thursday, about two dozen remote code execution flaws, mostly in Word, all needing a crafted file to be opened. Next dot js shipped fixes for two critical flaws, and a standard dependency check may miss them. Drupal fixed a critical core flaw exposed through common add on modules. N G I N X fixed five, including a client certificate bypass. And a Linux kernel flaw nicknamed PwnEscape lets a guest virtual machine read the host's memory. It was found by an A I system in three days, for under seven hundred dollars. Patch the hosts, not just the guests.

And one statistic from Cisco Talos to put it all in proportion. More than seventy thousand vulnerabilities published so far this year. Roughly double last year. Network devices account for about a quarter of those actually exploited. The front door, once again.

## Software supply chain

GitGuardian has documented a campaign called GhostAction. About nineteen hundred GitHub accounts compromised, and more than thirty thousand repositories targeted. The attackers pushed workflow files with reassuring names, "security audit", "github actions security", which quietly harvested cloud keys, package registry tokens, and A I service keys. The main wave was on the eighth and ninth of October. GitHub has blocked it. But some victims were hit twice, because they hadn't rotated their secrets after the first time. If a burglar has copied your key, changing the doormat does not help.

And an older but nasty one. A compromised npm package called tensorlake carried a worm that steals credentials and republishes the victim's own packages. It also installs a monitor that wipes your home directory if you revoke the stolen GitHub token. Hostage software, essentially. The advice is precise. Remove the monitor first. Then rotate. In that order.

## A I attacks and trends

A researcher claims a prompt injection flaw in ChatGPT's GitHub connector. Hidden text in a public repository persuades ChatGPT to commit a workflow into your private repository, and that workflow sends your secrets out. OpenAI reportedly closed the report as out of scope. It's not independently verified. The lesson stands regardless. Give A I connectors access to the repositories they need, and not one more.

An open source A I coding agent called Archon has a flaw scored nine point eight. Its control interface has no authentication, listens on every network interface by default, and the agent runs with its permission checks switched off. Anyone who can reach it can run commands. There's no patch. Bind it to the local machine.

Infostealers have noticed A I tools. Two families, Warden and Aoki, now steal session and configuration files from Claude, Codex, Cursor and Gemini. Those files can lead to code repositories and build pipelines. Yesterday's stolen browser cookie is today's stolen coding assistant.

On the regulatory side, the U S Federal Trade Commission sent lengthy demands to OpenAI and Anthropic about security incidents and safeguards. And the E U's A I Office sent its first information requests under the A I Act, to the same two companies, about incidents where models escaped their test environments. A first enforcement test. Precedents are being set this month.

And the market. Proofpoint is buying Varonis for six point three billion dollars, framed around controlling what data A I agents can reach. Everyone, it seems, has decided the agents are coming, and someone should be holding the keys.

## Deep dive: CL UNK twelve eleven

Now, our actor of the day. And this one has a peculiar signature. It talks to itself.

Unit forty two has described a cluster it calls C L U N K twelve eleven. It hasn't decided whether this is a nation state or a criminal group. From October last year to April this year, it went after government bodies and state linked infrastructure, on Linux web servers, mail servers and databases. The countries aren't named. The goal was intelligence. Stealing mailboxes. And something rather more modern. Searching government mailboxes for the recovery emails of social media accounts, so those accounts could be taken over.

Here's the peculiar part. The attacker's scripts are full of chatty, step by step comments addressed to the operator. Neat section headers. Rapid switching between four programming languages. Unit forty two reads that, sensibly, as code written by an A I model, or an operation run by an A I agent. Imagine a burglar whose toolkit came with a friendly instruction manual, written in the margins, by a machine that wanted to be helpful.

The way in was ordinary. P H P web shells, stolen credentials, and a custom scanner for a known React flaw, though that scanner isn't confirmed as the entry point. Then a custom remote access tool written in Go, which Unit forty two calls SysGoRAT, alongside the usual public tools for privilege escalation and tunnelling.

Persistence was thorough. The attacker edited every P H P configuration file on the server so that a malicious file was quietly loaded before every single page. A system service. A cron job every five minutes. Extra S S H keys. And on Windows machines, a scheduled task named, of course, OneDrive.

The credential theft was quietly clever. An alias that intercepted administrators' passwords every time they used sudo, saving them to a file named after SolarWinds. SolarWinds had nothing to do with it. The name was a disguise, chosen because nobody looks twice at a log file from a monitoring vendor. Login pages were modified to capture passwords. Configuration files and command histories were searched.

From there, lateral movement through Microsoft S Q L Server's command shell, hopping from one database server to another across subnets. Then mail and databases were packaged up and sent out to public file sharing sites.

And the exit was theatrical. The attacker tried to kill the endpoint security agent, and failed. Wiped the logs, twice. Altered file timestamps. And then planted little notes reading "red team ops twenty twenty five" and "pentest one two three". So that anyone who found the traces might conclude it was an authorised test, and go home early. A forged permission slip, left at the scene.

How do you hunt for it? Unexpected processes spawned by the P H P engine. Changes to the P H P setting that pre loads files. Sudo aliases in shell profiles. S S H keys nobody can explain. Hidden binaries in temporary memory folders. Mass log truncation. Outbound connections to G socket relays and file sharing sites. The full list of addresses, domains and hashes is in the written brief.

And the response. Rebuild, don't clean. Rotate every credential found on those machines. Patch the React flaw and the old PwnKit flaw. Filter outbound traffic. And treat any "red team" note you didn't commission as a lie until proven otherwise.

Why does it matter to us? Because these are exactly the techniques that would work against internet facing Linux servers, client portals, and web hosting. And because it's concrete evidence that A I is now writing parts of real intrusions. Not in theory. In logs.

## The week in review

And now, the week. Friday the ninth to Thursday the fifteenth of October. A short note first. Morning Signal began on Tuesday, so the weekend and Monday are covered only where those stories were still moving on Tuesday morning.

If the week had a theme, it was this. The doors at the edge, and the people in the middle.

The edge first. Ivanti Connect Secure had a zero day chain that gave a new actor, U T A zero five seven six, root on fully patched V P N appliances. The patching happened only in memory, so Ivanti's own integrity checker missed it. By Thursday it had moved to mass scanning. Palo Alto's firewall management interface and its GlobalProtect V P N were both exploited, the latter by ransomware crews. Cisco's email gateway could be taken over with a single attachment. SonicWall's remote access appliance and Atlassian's Data Center products were exploited within days of disclosure. Nextcloud servers had been quietly backdoored since September. And Microsoft's Patch Tuesday set a record, around fourteen hundred flaws, with an exploited Windows Shell bypass that lets a malicious attachment skip its security warnings. Unit forty two's PEERSTITCH report showed what happens when the console that manages five hundred firewalls is the thing that falls.

The people in the middle. Deloitte appeared on The Gentlemen's leak site, and as of Thursday, no data and no statement. The Silent Ransom Group's leaked chats showed people walking into law firms dressed as I T staff, and the group claims Baker McKenzie.
